# 08 — Animals & Protein 🏡

*Animals are the only part of a homestead that can turn things you cannot eat —
grass, weeds, kitchen scraps, insects, garden waste — into things you can.
That is their entire strategic purpose. Understand it, or you will build a system
that consumes more food than it produces.*

---

## 1. The feed problem — read this before buying a single chicken

Almost every plan for keeping animals in a crisis fails on the same arithmetic.

**Six laying hens:**
```
Feed:   6 hens × 130 g/day × 365 days   = 285 kg of grain per year
        285 kg × ~3,400 kcal/kg          ≈ 970,000 kcal IN

Eggs:   ~1,200 eggs/year × 70 kcal       ≈   84,000 kcal OUT
        plus spent hens and cockerels    ≈   30,000 kcal
                                           ------------
        Return on grain fed              ≈ 12%
```

**If you feed your chickens grain you could have eaten, you destroy about 88% of
those calories.** Six hens eat roughly what one adult eats. That is not a food
strategy — that is a luxury paid for in your own rations.

**Animals only make sense when they eat what you cannot:**

| Feed source | What it is | Who eats it |
|---|---|---|
| Kitchen and garden waste | Peelings, trimmings, spoiled produce, whey | Chickens, pigs, rabbits (greens only) |
| **Forage and pasture** | Grass, weeds, insects, worms, seeds | **Chickens (free-range), geese, goats, rabbits** |
| Crop residues | Bean haulm, brassica leaves, carrot tops, beet leaves | Rabbits, goats, chickens |
| **Fodder crops grown deliberately** | **Mangel-wurzel, fodder beet, turnip, kale, pumpkin, comfrey, lucerne, clover** | All of them — **this is the key link, and almost nobody plans it** |
| Hay | Cut and dried grass/clover | Rabbits, goats |
| Insects | **Black soldier fly larvae** or mealworms raised on food waste | Chickens, quail, fish — 40% protein, essentially free |
| Gleanings | Windfall fruit, spilled grain, spoiled stores, mill sweepings | All |
| Milk by-products | Whey, skimmed milk, buttermilk | Pigs, chickens |

**The rules that make livestock work:**
1. **Store feed for the animals as deliberately as for yourself.** If you keep
   chickens, store 6–12 months of feed (300–600 kg for six hens). Otherwise you
   will be eating your flock in month four.
2. **Grow fodder.** Mangel-wurzel yields 6–10 kg/m² and stores all winter in a
   clamp. 100 m² of mangels and fodder beet is a winter's feed for a flock and a
   couple of rabbits. Pumpkins store on a shelf and chickens love them. This
   single practice is what separates a renewable system from a countdown.
3. **Size the flock to the forage, not to your ambition.** In a real shortage,
   cull down to a breeding core. Six hens and a rooster can rebuild a flock of
   thirty in one season; thirty starving hens produce nothing.
4. **Maximise free-ranging.** A hen with genuine range for insects and greens
   can get 30–60% of her diet herself in summer, and the eggs are better.
5. **Composting and manure are part of the return.** Chicken manure fertilises
   the garden that feeds you. Count it.

## 3. Chickens — the household default

### Breed choice (this decides whether your flock renews)
- **Do NOT buy commercial hybrid layers** (ISA Brown, Lohmann, etc.) as your only
  stock. They lay superbly for two years and then collapse, and **they have had
  broodiness bred out of them** — they will never hatch a chick. A flock of
  hybrids is a consumable, not a system.
- **Buy dual-purpose heritage breeds that go broody:** Orpington, Sussex,
  Wyandotte, Marans, Australorp, Barnevelder, Plymouth Rock, or a good local
  landrace. Fewer eggs (180–220/yr), better meat on the cockerels, and — the
  critical part — **a hen that will sit on eggs and raise chicks for you, for
  free, without electricity.**
- **A broody hen is a solar-powered incubator that also raises the chicks.** This
  is the single most important livestock decision in this chapter.
- Keep **a rooster** if the law and your neighbours allow. Without one, there are
  no fertile eggs and no next generation. If a rooster is impossible, keep an
  incubator + a source of fertile eggs, and accept the power dependency.

### Numbers
- **4–6 hens per 4-person household** for eggs. 6 hens ≈ 3–5 eggs/day in season.
- Add **2–4 more** if you want meat as well as eggs.
- Winter laying drops to near zero in December–January without supplementary
  light. **14 hours of daylight** keeps them laying — a 5 W LED on a timer in the
  coop (a trivial electrical load) restores winter production. Whether to do this
  is a real trade-off: it shortens the hen's productive life, but it puts protein
  on the table in the hardest months.

### Housing
- **Coop: 0.4 m²/bird minimum**, dry, draught-free but **ventilated at the top**
  (damp, not cold, is what kills chickens), with perches (20 cm/bird) and one
  nest box per 4 hens.
- **Run: 2 m²/bird absolute minimum, 10 m²+ if you want real forage value.**
  A bare, overgrazed run is a mud-and-parasite problem; rotating two runs, or a
  movable tractor on grass, keeps it healthy.
- **Predator-proof it properly.** Fox, marten, rat, hawk, dog. Weld mesh (not
  chicken wire — a fox goes through chicken wire), buried or skirted 30 cm,
  a secure roof, and a door that closes every single night. **Losing the flock to
  a fox in month two is the classic, avoidable disaster.** An automatic door is
  fine, but keep a manual latch.
- Deep litter (wood shavings/straw built up over months) composts in place, keeps
  the coop warm, and produces excellent garden compost. Clean out twice a year.

### Feeding
- Layer feed: **120–150 g/bird/day.** Store 6–12 months (see §1).
- **Grit** (insoluble, for the gizzard) and **oyster shell/crushed eggshell**
  (calcium, for shells) — both essential, both store indefinitely, both cheap.
  **Stock several years' worth; a hen without calcium lays soft-shelled eggs and
  then stops.**
- Supplement with: kitchen scraps, garden waste, comfrey, cut grass, windfall
  fruit, spent brewing grain, mangels and pumpkins in winter, and insect larvae.
- **Fodder sprouting** (barley sprouted in trays for 7 days, 1 kg of grain →
  6–8 kg of green fodder) — be clear about what this does: it does **not** create
  calories (dry matter actually falls slightly), but it converts stored dry grain
  into **fresh green feed with vitamins** in winter, which is exactly what a
  confined flock lacks. Useful, not magic.
- **Black soldier fly larvae**: a simple bucket bioreactor turns kitchen waste
  into 40%-protein larvae that self-harvest by crawling up a ramp. One of the best
  closed-loop tricks available.
- Water: 0.25–0.5 L/bird/day, and **it must not freeze.** A frozen drinker is the
  most common winter chicken killer. Keep a spare and rotate it from the house.

### The renewal cycle — how a flock becomes permanent
```
March–June   Broody hen sits on 8–12 fertile eggs → 21 days → chicks
             (or set eggs under her from your best layers)
Weeks 0–8    Hen raises chicks; ~50% will be cockerels
Weeks 16–20  Cockerels reach 1.5–2.5 kg → slaughter all but one or two
Month 5–6    Pullets start laying
Autumn       Cull the oldest third of the hens (years 3+, laying declines) — into
             the pot, the stockpot, or the canner
Result       Flock size stable, age profile young, genetics refreshed,
             meat on the table twice a year, and NO purchases required
```
**Replace about one third of your layers each year.** Hens lay best in years 1–2,
decline from year 3, and are still perfectly good stewing birds (old hens make
the best stock there is — long, slow cooking, or pressure-canned).

### Health
Common problems: red mite (check perches weekly — treat with diatomaceous earth
and a thorough clean), worms, scaly leg mite, coccidiosis in chicks, respiratory
infection from damp housing. Keep the coop dry and ventilated, rotate the ground,
quarantine any new bird for 3 weeks, and cull sick birds early rather than letting
disease run through the flock. **Stock diatomaceous earth, poultry tonic and a
sharp knife.**

## 4. Rabbits — the most underrated survival animal

Rabbits deserve far more attention than they get in most plans.

**Why:** silent (no rooster problem), small footprint, breed prolifically, eat
grass, weeds, hay and garden waste rather than grain, and reach eating size in
10–12 weeks. A trio of does and one buck occupies about 4 m² and produces
**80–120 kg of meat a year**.

```
Gestation 31 days · 6–8 kits per litter · 4–5 litters/doe/year
3 does × 4 litters × 7 kits × 1.25 kg meat each ≈ 105 kg meat/year
```

- **Feed:** hay (the staple — they need it constantly for gut health), fresh
  greens, garden waste, carrots and roots, comfrey, dandelion, plantain, bramble
  leaves. Pellets are convenient but not essential — which is exactly the point.
  Introduce fresh greens gradually; a sudden change causes fatal gut problems.
- **Housing:** hutches off the ground, dry, shaded in summer (rabbits tolerate
  cold far better than heat — above 28 °C is dangerous), with wire floors over a
  dropping tray, or colony housing on deep litter. Does need a nest box before
  kindling.
- **Manure is "cold"** — it can go straight onto beds with no composting. It is
  excellent, and a genuine convenience advantage over poultry.
- **Breeding:** take the doe to the buck (never the reverse — she'll defend her
  territory). Keep records: mating date, kindling date, litter size, weaning.
  Rest each doe properly; don't breed her to death.
- ⚠️ **Rabbit is extremely lean (~1,300 kcal/kg, almost no fat).** A diet of
  rabbit alone causes **protein poisoning ("rabbit starvation")** — fatal within
  weeks. Always eat rabbit with fat and carbohydrate: lard, oil, potatoes, grain.
  **This is a real historical cause of death and it must be designed around.**
- Slaughter is quick and requires no equipment beyond a sharp knife and a hook —
  the easiest livestock for a beginner to process humanely. Learn it properly
  from someone experienced before you need to.

## 5. Quail — the small-space option

- **Coturnix (Japanese) quail lay at 6–8 weeks old** — the fastest protein of any
  livestock — and produce 250–300 eggs a year each.
- **5 quail eggs ≈ 1 chicken egg.** A dozen birds gives a family a real egg supply.
- **0.1 m²/bird**: 10 birds live comfortably in 1 m², indoors or on a balcony.
- Quiet (the male's call is soft and far less carrying than a cockerel's).
- ⚠️ **They do not go broody.** Domesticated coturnix have lost the instinct —
  so **renewal requires an incubator** (a small one is €60 and about 40 W for
  17 days, well within a modest solar system, but it is a dependency).
- Need higher protein feed than chickens (game-bird crumb, ~20–24% protein);
  insects and sprouted seed help.
- Meat birds at 8–10 weeks, ~200 g dressed. Small but fast.
- **The realistic apartment livestock.** See Ch. 09.

## 6. Larger animals — only with land and commitment

**Goats (dairy):** 2 does give 2–4 L of milk a day for 250–300 days a year — milk,
cheese, butter, yoghurt, and whey for the pigs and chickens. But: they need real
browse or a lot of hay (1–2 kg/day each in winter), fencing that actually holds
them (they are escape artists), annual breeding to keep milking, and **twice-daily
milking every single day without exception.** That last point defeats most people.
Sheep are easier but give less milk; a house cow gives far more milk and eats far
more.

**Pigs:** the best calorie and **fat** converter there is, and fat is the hardest
thing to produce at home. A pig eats 2–3 kg/day and reaches 100 kg live in 6–8
months, yielding ~70 kg of carcass including **15 kg of lard** — which solves the
cooking-fat problem that nothing else on a homestead solves. They will eat
almost anything: whey, spoiled produce, windfalls, acorns, waste. Two pigs raised
over a summer on surplus and waste, slaughtered in autumn, then cured and canned,
is the classic peasant economy for good reason. **Check the law: feeding
household/catering food waste to pigs is illegal in the EU and UK** (disease
control), so plan feed you can legally use.

**Ducks:** better foragers than chickens, lay well, eat slugs voraciously, are
hardier in wet weather, and don't destroy a garden the way chickens do. They need
water to dip their heads in (not necessarily a pond) and they make mud. Muscovies
are quiet, broody and excellent mothers. **Seriously consider ducks over chickens
if your site is wet.**

**Geese:** live almost entirely on grass, guard the yard, and provide a large
carcass and a lot of fat — but they are loud and aggressive, which is either a
security feature or a neighbour problem.

**Bees:** 15–30 kg of honey per hive per year, plus wax for candles and salves,
plus pollination that lifts fruit yields across the whole plot. Low daily effort
once established, but real skill is needed and there is a €300–600 setup cost
(hive, suit, smoker, tools, extractor — or share an extractor with your group).
Honey never spoils. **The best effort-to-value ratio on a homestead** — start
learning from a local association now, because beekeeping is not a thing you can
learn from a book in a crisis.

**Fish / aquaponics:** high complexity and it fails hard. Fish need constant
aeration (a pump = continuous power, and a failure kills everything in hours),
stable temperature, and feed that is usually bought in. A **low-tech farm pond**
stocked with carp or tench, fed on kitchen waste and duckweed, is a far more
robust proposition than an aquaponics rig — much lower yield per m², but it
survives a blackout.

## 7. Slaughter, butchery and preservation

**This is a skill, and it must be learned before you need it.** Find a local
smallholder, a hunting club, or a course. Doing it badly is cruel, unsafe and
wasteful; doing it well is quick, calm and respectful.

- **Learn the humane methods** for your species and follow the legal requirements
  in your country (most permit home slaughter of your own poultry and rabbits for
  your own household; larger animals usually require a licensed abattoir or a
  licensed slaughterman — check).
- **Equipment:** sharp knives (and a steel and a whetstone — a blunt knife is the
  main cause of both cruelty and injury), a killing cone for poultry, a gambrel or
  hooks, a scalding pot (poultry plucking: 60–65 °C for 30–60 s), a plucking
  table, buckets, and a clean cool space.
- **Use everything:** meat, offal (liver and heart are the most nutrient-dense
  food available to you — do not waste them), fat (**render it: lard and tallow
  are precious**), bones (long-simmered stock, then to the compost or the dogs),
  blood (black pudding), skin, feathers (pillows and insulation), and the
  intestines if you are making sausage.
- **Preserve immediately** (Ch. 03 §6): pressure canning is the best
  power-independent option for meat and stock — jars of chicken and stock on a
  shelf need no freezer and no electricity. Then salting, curing, smoking,
  drying, and confit.
- **Timing:** the traditional autumn slaughter exists because the animal is fat
  from the summer, the weather is cold enough to work safely, and you are not
  feeding it through the winter. Keep breeding stock; eat the rest.

## 8. Legal and neighbourly

- Check municipal rules on poultry and livestock in residential areas —
  **roosters are the usual sticking point** and are banned or restricted in many
  urban areas.
- Registration requirements (many countries require a holding number for
  livestock, and movement/medicine records).
- Slaughter law, waste and carcass disposal law, manure storage rules.
- **Talk to your neighbours before, not after.** Noise, smell and flies are
  manageable with good husbandry, and a neighbour who gets a dozen eggs a week is
  an ally rather than a complainant. This is also the beginning of your mutual-aid
  group (Ch. 10).

## 9. Build sequence

| Level | Land track |
|---|---|
| **Now, €0** | Find a local keeper and visit · join a poultry/beekeeping association · read one good book per species · check your local rules |
| **L1, €400–700** | Coop + secure run built · 4–6 heritage hens · feeders, drinkers, grit, oyster shell · 3 months of feed |
| **L2, €800–1,500** | + a rooster · 6–12 months of feed stored · fodder crops (mangels, pumpkins, kale) planted · rabbit trio + hutches · black soldier fly bucket · first broody hatch completed |
| **L3, €2,000+** | + bees · ducks or geese · pigs raised over a summer · goats if you have pasture · pressure canner and full butchery kit · a second breeding line, or a chick/kit exchange agreement with your group (genetics matter — a closed flock degrades) |

## 10. Animals checklist

```
[ ] I have calculated the annual feed requirement in kg — and where it comes from
[ ] 6–12 months of feed stored, plus grit and oyster shell for years
[ ] Fodder crops (mangel, fodder beet, pumpkin, kale, comfrey) planted for winter
[ ] Breeds chosen that go broody / breed without an incubator
[ ] A rooster (or a fertile-egg plan) — the flock can actually renew
[ ] Housing is predator-proof with weld mesh, buried skirt, and a nightly door
[ ] Ventilation without draughts; dry bedding; deep litter started
[ ] Water that will not freeze in January, plus a spare drinker
[ ] Free-range or rotational area to cut the feed bill
[ ] Black soldier fly or mealworm bucket running
[ ] Rabbits considered seriously (quiet, forage-fed, high output)
[ ] Fat source planned — rabbit alone is a trap
[ ] Slaughter and butchery learned from a real person, with sharp tools ready
[ ] Preservation ready before the first slaughter (canner, jars, salt, smoker)
[ ] Local law checked: roosters, registration, slaughter, waste
[ ] Neighbours spoken to; eggs shared
[ ] Cull-down plan written for a genuine feed shortage (breeding core defined)
```
