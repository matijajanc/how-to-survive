# 07 — Land & Garden 🏡

*Growing food is the only preparation that renews itself. It is also slow,
skill-dependent, and unforgiving of being started late. **Plant the first bed
this month, whatever month it is.***

---

## 1. The honest expectations

**Read this before you plan anything.**

1. **Year one will disappoint you.** Your soil is poor, your timing is wrong,
   your slugs are numerous, and you don't yet know your site. Expect 25–40% of
   the yield an experienced gardener gets from the same ground. This is normal
   and it is not a reason to stop — it is the reason to start *now*, while
   failure costs nothing but a season.
2. **Vegetables are not calories.** A gorgeous bed of lettuce, tomatoes and
   courgettes is worth maybe 200–600 kcal/m²/year. It will not feed you. It
   provides the vitamins, minerals, flavour and morale that make your **stored
   staples** survivable. **Storage feeds you; the garden keeps you healthy and
   sane.** Design around that division of labour.
3. **The garden is a supplement in year one, a substantial contributor in year
   three, and a foundation in year five.** Perennials and soil take that long.
4. **Full self-sufficiency needs land most people don't have.** See the table
   below. Aim for meaningful contribution, not autarky.
5. **Preservation capacity, not growing capacity, is the real limit.** Anyone can
   grow 60 kg of tomatoes in August. Having them in February requires jars, a
   canner, a dehydrator and a cellar. Build the preserving side in parallel
   (Ch. 03 §6).

## 2. How much land do you actually need?

| Goal | Area per person | What it looks like |
|---|---|---|
| Fresh vegetables and herbs in season, big morale/vitamin value | **30 – 50 m²** | Four raised beds and some pots |
| Above, plus enough to preserve for winter vegetables | **80 – 150 m²** | A serious kitchen garden |
| **Above, plus a meaningful calorie contribution (30–50% of calories)** | **250 – 400 m²** | Kitchen garden + a potato/squash/bean plot |
| Most calories, most of the year, still buying oil/grain/salt | **600 – 1,000 m²** | A smallholding plot, plus a cellar and a polytunnel |
| Genuine full food self-sufficiency incl. grain, oil, and animal feed | **1,500 – 4,000 m²** (0.15–0.4 ha) | A small farm, with animals, and it is a full-time job |

**Add for animals** (Ch. 08): chickens need 10–20 m² of run for a small flock but
**far more land than that to feed** — a hectare of grain feeds a lot of chickens
and 6 hens eat ~300 kg of grain a year. Pasture for a goat: 1,000–2,000 m².

**Plan your actual site:**
```
Total plot                 ______ m²
  minus house/paths/shed   ______ m²
  minus too shaded (<5 h summer sun) ______ m²
  minus too wet/steep/rocky          ______ m²
= USABLE GROWING AREA      ______ m²
  of which: annual beds ____   perennial/fruit ____   animals ____   wood/coppice ____
```

## 3. Calorie-dense crops — what to actually grow

Ranked by realistic home-garden calories per square metre per year, with storage
behaviour, which matters just as much.

| Crop | kg/m² | kcal/m² | Stores how long | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Potato** | 3 – 5 | **2,300 – 3,800** | 5–8 months in a dark cellar at 4–8 °C | **The single best survival crop in this climate.** Easy, fast, calorie-dense, stores well, and you replant from your own tubers. Watch for blight — grow resistant varieties (Sarpo Mira/Axona are outstanding). |
| **Winter squash / pumpkin** | 3 – 5 | 1,200 – 2,000 | **4–8 months at 10–15 °C, no cellar needed** | Enormous value: it stores at *room temperature* on a shelf. Also a fine animal feed. Needs space and a long season. |
| **Jerusalem artichoke** | 2 – 4 | 1,500 – 2,900 | In the ground all winter — dig as needed | Bombproof, perennial, near-zero effort, thrives in poor soil. Two warnings: it is invasive (site it where you don't mind), and inulin causes serious flatulence until the gut adapts. |
| **Maize (grain/flour corn)** | 0.5 – 0.8 | 1,800 – 2,900 | Years, dried on the cob | Needs sun and a long season; dries and stores beautifully; feeds people and poultry. Grow a flour/flint variety, not sweetcorn. |
| **Beetroot / mangel-wurzel** | 4 – 6 | 1,700 – 2,500 | 4–6 months in sand | Mangels are a classic winter **animal fodder** — a critical link in the livestock chain. |
| **Sweet potato** | 2 – 3 | 1,700 – 2,600 | 4–6 months, warm and dry | Needs warmth; increasingly viable in Central Europe with fleece and a warm site. |
| **Carrot / parsnip** | 3 – 5 | 1,300 – 2,000 | 4–6 months in damp sand; parsnips overwinter in the ground | Parsnip is a hungry-gap hero and sweetens after frost |
| **Onion / garlic** | 2 – 4 | 800 – 1,600 | **6–9 months, hanging, dry and cool** | Low calories, high value: flavour is what makes a storage diet edible. Garlic is also medicinal and near-effortless. |
| **Cabbage** (incl. for sauerkraut) | 4 – 6 | 1,000 – 1,500 | 3–5 months whole; **12 months as sauerkraut** | Vitamin C through winter. Grow a lot. |
| **Kale / chard / leaf beet** | 2 – 4 | 400 – 900 | Standing crop, harvested through winter | **Stands in the ground at −10 °C and feeds you in January.** Essential. |
| **Dry beans** | 0.15 – 0.3 | 500 – 1,000 | **Years** | Low yield per m² but **protein + stores indefinitely + fixes nitrogen + easy to save seed**. Grow climbing varieties for yield per m². |
| **Peas (dry)** | 0.15 – 0.25 | 500 – 850 | Years | As above, and earlier in the season |
| **Sunflower** | 0.2 – 0.3 seed | ~1,500 (as oil/seed) | Seed: 1 yr; oil: 1 yr | The realistic home oil crop, but **pressing needs a press** (a hand oil press is €250–600 and low-yielding). Also excellent chicken feed. |
| **Wheat / rye / barley** | 0.3 – 0.5 | 1,000 – 1,700 | **Years as whole grain** | Poor use of a small garden (you'd need ~500 m² per person for bread alone), but worth growing on any spare field area, and rye will grow on poor soil. Threshing by hand is laborious. |
| **Turnip / swede / kohlrabi** | 3 – 5 | 900 – 1,500 | 4–5 months | Fast, cold-hardy, doubles as fodder |
| Tomato, courgette, cucumber, pepper, salad | 3 – 10 | 200 – 600 | Fresh only; preserve | Low calories, very high vitamin, flavour and morale value. **Grow them — just don't count them as food security.** |

### The allocation rule for a survival garden
```
50–60%  calorie crops    (potatoes, squash, maize, roots, beans)
20–25%  storage/winter   (cabbage, kale, leeks, onions, garlic, parsnips)
15–20%  fresh/vitamin    (tomatoes, salad, cucumbers, peppers, herbs)
 5%     experiments      (you must keep learning what works on YOUR site)
```

## 4. Laying out the garden

### Beds
- **1.2 m wide, any length**, with **45–60 cm paths.** You can reach the middle
  from either side, so you never step on the soil. Compacted soil is the most
  common cause of poor yields.
- Orient rows **north–south** where possible so plants don't shade each other.
- Raised beds (15–25 cm of soil above path level, edged or not) warm earlier in
  spring, drain better, and are easier on the back. On dry sites, sunken beds
  conserve water instead.
- **No-dig / no-till:** cover the ground with cardboard, then 5–10 cm of compost
  on top, and plant into it. It suppresses weeds, feeds soil life, halves the
  labour, and after 2–3 years gives better yields than digging. **This is the
  method to use** — especially when you have no fuel, no machinery and limited
  calories to spend on labour.

### Rotation — 4 beds, 4 years
Rotation prevents the build-up of soil-borne disease and balances nutrient
demand. Move each group one bed along each year:

```
Bed 1  LEGUMES      peas, beans           → fix nitrogen, leave it behind
Bed 2  BRASSICAS    cabbage, kale, swede  → use that nitrogen; lime if needed
Bed 3  ROOTS/ALLIUM carrot, parsnip, beet, onion, leek → low nitrogen demand
Bed 4  POTATOES/SOLANACEAE + squash       → heavy feeders, get the compost
```
Never grow potatoes or tomatoes in the same ground two years running (blight and
eelworm). Onions and brassicas both benefit from a 3–4 year gap.

### Protected growing — the highest-value structure you can build
A **polytunnel or greenhouse changes your calendar by 6–8 weeks at each end** and
makes winter salad possible.
- A 3 × 6 m polytunnel costs €400–900 and is the best value; a glasshouse is
  prettier, more fragile, and more expensive.
- **Cold frames** (a glazed lid on a box, €40 or free from old windows) do most
  of the same job at 1/20th of the cost, for hardening off, winter salad,
  early sowing and overwintering.
- **Fleece and cloches** (€30) buy 2–4 weeks at each end of the season and
  protect against late frosts — the cheapest season extension there is.
- Aim for: seedlings in a cold frame from February, tomatoes/peppers/cucumbers
  in the tunnel from May, and winter salad/spinach/claytonia in the tunnel
  November–March.

## 5. The hungry gap — the thing that catches everyone out

**March, April and May are the hardest months**, not winter. Stores are exhausted,
last year's roots are sprouting, and this year's crops are barely seedlings.
Historically this is when people starved.

**Design for it explicitly:**

| Strategy | What to do |
|---|---|
| **Overwintering standing crops** | Kale, leeks, parsnips, swede, Brussels sprouts, chard, claytonia, corn salad, land cress, sprouting broccoli (**purple sprouting broccoli crops in exactly April** — plant it) |
| **Long-storing roots** | Onions and garlic (into May/June), potatoes (to May if cellared properly), carrots in sand, beetroot |
| **Autumn sowings** | Broad beans and garlic sown October–November crop far earlier than spring sowings |
| **The polytunnel** | Winter salad, spinach, radish, early carrots |
| **Preserves** | This is what the jars and sauerkraut are *for* — the gap, not the autumn glut |
| **Sprouts and microgreens** | 5–14 days to fresh food, indoors, any month. **The hungry gap insurance policy** (Ch. 09) |
| **Perennials** | Rhubarb (April), asparagus (April–June), sorrel, Welsh onion, wild garlic, nettle tops, dandelion — most of the earliest food in the year is perennial |
| **Stored calories** | Keep your grain and bean store as the explicit March–May buffer, and ration to it |

## 6. Seed — the true renewable

**Seed is what turns one garden into an infinite number of gardens.** Without
saved seed you are running a countdown timer, not a system.

### Buy the right kind
- **Open-pollinated (OP) or heirloom varieties ONLY** for anything you intend to
  save. **F1 hybrids do not come true from saved seed** — the offspring are a
  variable mess. F1s are often more vigorous and disease-resistant, so keep a few,
  but your strategic seed store must be OP.
- **Buy locally-adapted varieties** where you can; they are already suited to your
  climate and diseases.
- **Store a 3–5 year seed bank.** Seed is tiny, cheap and light. A comprehensive
  seed store for a 300 m² garden costs **€150–300** and fits in a shoebox. There
  is no better value per gram in this whole plan.
- Buy extra of the things you cannot easily save: F1-only varieties you rely on,
  and biennials.

### Seed viability (years, stored cool, dry and dark)
| 1–2 yrs | 3 yrs | 4–6 yrs |
|---|---|---|
| Onion, leek, parsnip, salsify, sweetcorn | Carrot, pea, bean, pepper, spinach, chives | Tomato, cabbage/brassicas, beetroot, cucumber, squash, melon, lettuce, radish, turnip |

**Store seed** in paper envelopes inside a sealed glass jar with a silica gel
sachet or a spoonful of dry rice, in the fridge or a cold cellar. Cold + dry
roughly doubles viability. Label with variety and year. **Test germination
annually**: 10 seeds on a damp paper towel in a bag — count how many sprout in
10–14 days.

### Saving seed — start with the easy ones
**Easy (self-pollinating, minimal isolation, save from day one):**
tomato, French/runner bean, pea, lettuce, pepper (mostly), and any
self-fertile herb. Save from these in your first year. Take seed from your
**best plants**, not your leftovers — the ones that were healthiest, earliest and
tastiest. That is how you breed a strain adapted to your own garden.
- Tomato: scoop seeds + pulp into a jar of water, ferment 2–3 days until it
  moults, rinse, dry on a plate (not paper).
- Beans and peas: leave the pods on the plant until they rattle and are papery.
- Lettuce: let it bolt and flower, collect the fluffy seed heads.

**Harder (cross-pollinating — needs isolation distance or hand-pollination):**
- Squash/pumpkin: cross freely **within the same species**. Grow only one variety
  per species, or hand-pollinate and tape the flowers.
- Maize: wind-pollinated, needs 300 m+ separation or different flowering times.
  Save from **at least 100–200 plants** or the strain degrades from inbreeding.
- Cucumber, melon: 400 m+ or hand-pollinate.

**Hardest (biennial — they flower in their second year, so you must overwinter
the plant):** carrot, beetroot, cabbage and all brassicas, onion, leek, parsnip
(parsnip is actually easy — it self-seeds readily), turnip, swede.
Brassicas also cross-pollinate wildly with each other and need 800 m+ or cages.
**Plan to buy these in, or pick just one biennial to master per year.**

### Also store
Seed potatoes (save your own from healthy, disease-free plants — after a few
years buy in certified stock to reset virus load), garlic cloves, onion sets,
and root cuttings from your perennials.

## 7. Fertility without a supply chain

The moment fertiliser deliveries stop, your garden lives on what you can close
the loop on. This is the difference between a garden and a mine.

**A vegetable garden needs roughly 10–20 g of nitrogen per m² per year**, plus
potassium, phosphorus, calcium and trace elements, plus organic matter to hold
water and feed soil life.

| Source | What it gives | How to use it |
|---|---|---|
| **Compost** | Everything, slowly, plus organic matter | The foundation. Build **two or three bays** (1 m³ each): fill one, turn into the next, use the third. Mix ~2:1 browns (straw, leaves, card, wood chip) to greens (kitchen waste, weeds, grass, manure). Keep damp and turn it. A hot pile finishes in 3 months; a cold one in 12. **You cannot have too much compost.** |
| **Urine** | Nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium — a genuinely balanced fertiliser | **Dilute 1:8–1:10**, water onto soil around growing plants. One adult's output ≈ enough N for **200–300 m²/year**. Free, sterile, and the biggest single fertility input available to most households (Ch. 05). |
| **Humanure compost** | Everything + organic matter | Two-year composting, then fruit trees and non-food crops only (Ch. 05) |
| **Animal manure** | N, P, K + organic matter | **Chicken manure is hot** — compost it 6+ months or it burns plants. Rabbit manure is *cold* and can go straight on beds — a real advantage. Horse/cow manure: compost, and beware aminopyralid herbicide residues that destroy tomatoes and beans (test a sample on a bean seedling before spreading a load). |
| **Green manures / cover crops** | Nitrogen, organic matter, weed suppression, erosion control | Sow on any bare bed. **Field beans, vetch, clover, lupin** fix 50–200 kg N/ha. Phacelia, mustard and rye add bulk and protect soil over winter. Chop down before they seed and leave on the surface or dig in shallowly. **Never leave soil bare over winter.** |
| **Comfrey (Bocking 14)** | Potassium, especially — perfect for tomatoes, potatoes and fruit | Plant a dozen roots in a corner. Cut 3–4 times a year; use as mulch, in the compost, or as a liquid feed (leaves in a barrel of water for 4 weeks — it smells appalling and works brilliantly). Sterile Bocking 14 doesn't seed and take over. |
| **Wood ash** | Potassium + calcium, raises pH | From your wood stove. A light dusting only — it is strongly alkaline. **Never on potatoes** (encourages scab) or on acid-loving plants. Keep it dry until used. |
| **Nettles** | Nitrogen | Liquid feed, same method as comfrey. Free, everywhere. |
| **Biochar** | Nothing itself — a permanent habitat for microbes and a water/nutrient sponge | Charcoal from a smothered fire, crushed, **charged** by soaking in urine or compost tea before use. A genuine long-term soil improvement, and a way to use woody waste. |
| **Leaf mould** | Organic matter, water retention | Pile autumn leaves in a wire cage, wait 1–2 years. Free, effortless. |
| **Lime** | Calcium, raises pH | Buy a few sacks now. Brassicas want pH 6.5–7.5 and clubroot is suppressed by lime. It does not perish. |
| **Rock dust, bone meal, seaweed** | Trace elements, phosphorus | Worth stocking a sack of each — they store indefinitely and cover what the loop can't. |

**Test your soil pH now (€10 kit).** Most vegetables want 6.0–7.0. Getting pH
wrong locks out nutrients no matter how much fertility you add.

**Mulch everything.** Straw, leaves, grass clippings, cardboard, compost. Mulch
halves your watering, suppresses weeds, feeds the soil and prevents erosion.
It is free labour-saving and the single most useful habit in a low-input garden.

## 8. Water in the garden

- **Mulch first** — it saves more water than any irrigation system.
- **Water deeply and infrequently** (once or twice a week, thoroughly), not a
  little every day. Deep water grows deep roots that survive drought.
- **Water at the base, in the morning or evening.** Overhead watering in sun
  wastes most of it and spreads fungal disease.
- **Drip line or soaker hose** from a rainwater tank on a stand — gravity gives
  enough pressure for soaker hose at 1 m of head. €60 for a garden's worth.
- **Ollas** (unglazed clay pots buried to the neck, filled weekly) water roots
  directly with near-zero loss. Ancient, effective, and free if you have old pots.
- **Swales and shaped beds** on a slope slow and sink runoff instead of losing it.
- **Prioritise ruthlessly in a drought:** water what you cannot replace
  (fruit trees, perennials, seedlings, and the crops you eat in winter). Let
  salad die. Squash and potatoes need water at flowering and tuber-set; mature
  roots and brassicas will survive a lot of neglect.
- **Rainwater, greywater and stored water — never drinking water on the garden.**
  See Ch. 01.

## 9. Pests and disease without sprays

- **Physical barriers are 90% of the answer.** Insect mesh over brassicas (against
  cabbage white and root fly), fleece over carrots (carrot fly flies low — a
  60 cm barrier works), netting over fruit, collars around brassica stems.
  **Buy plenty of mesh and netting now** — it lasts years and there is no
  substitute.
- **Slugs** are the number one garden enemy in a wet Central European spring.
  Beer traps, night-time hand collection (genuinely the most effective method),
  a duck or two (superb slug hunters — Indian Runners), removing hiding places,
  copper tape on pots, coarse wool pellets, and starting plants big in modules
  rather than direct sowing.
- **Blight** (potato/tomato) arrives in warm wet July weather. Grow resistant
  varieties, space for airflow, avoid overhead watering, earth up potatoes,
  and **cut and remove the haulm the moment blight appears** — the tubers keep
  growing and stay clean underground.
- **Rotation and hygiene**: 4-year rotation, remove diseased material (burn it,
  don't compost it), clean tools, don't work among wet plants.
- **Encourage predators:** a pond (frogs eat slugs), flowers for hoverflies and
  lacewings (poached egg plant, phacelia, calendula, umbellifers), hedges and log
  piles for hedgehogs and beetles, bird boxes. A garden with a healthy predator
  population has far fewer pest explosions.
- **Companion planting and interplanting:** partly folklore, but strong-smelling
  plants (onions among carrots, marigolds, herbs) genuinely confuse some pests,
  and mixed plantings limit how fast an outbreak spreads.
- **Larger pests:** fence against deer (1.8 m) and rabbits (buried 30 cm, 1 m
  high); net against birds; trap rodents around the store. **Fencing is a
  capital item — buy and install it before you need it.**

## 10. Perennials — plant them NOW, they take years

This is the most time-sensitive item in the entire plan, because there is no way
to shortcut it. A tree planted today fruits in 3–8 years. A tree planted at AMBER
fruits never.

| Plant | First crop | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Strawberries** | **Year 1** | Fastest fruit there is. Multiply free from runners. |
| **Raspberries** (summer + autumn varieties) | Year 2 | Very productive, spreads itself, freezes/jams well |
| **Currants (black/red/white), gooseberries, jostaberry** | Year 2–3 | Tough, reliable, high in vitamin C, propagate free from hardwood cuttings |
| **Rhubarb** | Year 2 | The earliest garden food of the year; near-immortal |
| **Asparagus** | Year 3 | 20+ year crop once established |
| **Apples, pears, plums, cherries** (on semi-dwarfing rootstock) | Year 3–6 | **The backbone of a food-secure property.** Choose storing varieties — some apples keep to March in a cellar. Plant several varieties for pollination and to spread the season. |
| **Grapes** | Year 3 | Fresh, dried (raisins store for a year), juice, vinegar, wine |
| **Elderberry, rosehip, sea buckthorn** | Year 2–4 | Huge vitamin C, medicinal, thrive on neglect. Sea buckthorn is one of the richest vitamin C sources that grows here. |
| **Hazelnuts** | Year 4–6 | **Fat and protein that stores** — the most valuable nut for this climate, and it coppices for firewood |
| **Walnuts** | Year 8–15 | Plant anyway. Fat, protein, stores in the shell for a year, and superb timber. |
| **Herbs** (rosemary, thyme, sage, oregano, mint, bay) | Year 1 | Flavour, tea, and medicine, from almost no space |
| **Comfrey, sorrel, Welsh/walking onion, wild garlic, Good King Henry, sea kale** | Year 1–2 | Perennial vegetables — they crop in the hungry gap and need almost no work |
| **Willow / hazel coppice** | Year 3–7 | Renewable firewood, poles, bean sticks, basketry. A coppice on 500 m² gives a sustainable annual cut forever. |

**Do this in the next planting season, whatever else is unfinished.** Bare-root
trees and bushes in November–March are cheap (€10–25 each); the whole list above
for a garden costs €300–600 and is the highest-compounding investment here.

## 11. Tools for a garden that has to work

**Hand tools first, and buy quality — these should last 30 years:**
spade · digging fork · rake · hoe (Dutch/push hoe and a draw hoe) ·
trowel and hand fork · secateurs (+ a spare) · loppers · pruning saw ·
garden line · wheelbarrow · watering cans (2 × 10 L) · buckets ·
sharpening stone and a file · a good knife.

**Then:** seed trays and modules · pots · propagator or a warm windowsill ·
labels and a pencil (ink fades) · fleece, mesh, netting · canes and string ·
compost bins · hose and soaker line · a scythe (genuinely practical for grass,
green manures and fodder, and needs no fuel — but needs learning and peening) ·
a broadfork · a garden cart · a soil thermometer · a moisture meter · a
notebook.

**Spares and consumables:** twine, wire, staples, spare mesh, spare fleece,
replacement tool handles, tool oil, glass or polythene for the frames/tunnel
(**polytunnel plastic lasts 5–7 years — buy the replacement sheet now**).

**The garden notebook is a tool.** Record what you sowed, when, which variety,
what happened, what the weather did, what you harvested and when. Three years of
notes is worth more than any book, because it is about *your* site.

## 12. Year one — a realistic first-season plan

Don't try to do everything. Do this:

**Autumn/winter (start here whenever you're reading this)**
1. Mark out **four beds of 1.2 m × 5 m** (24 m² of growing space). That is
   enough to learn on and not enough to defeat you.
2. Cardboard + 10 cm of compost on top. No digging.
3. Order seed (OP varieties), compost, and bare-root fruit.
4. Plant the fruit trees, bushes, rhubarb and asparagus **now** — they are on the
   longest clock.
5. Build a compost bay. Start filling it.
6. Plant garlic and broad beans (October–November) for an early crop.

**Spring**
7. Sow in modules on a windowsill or in a cold frame: brassicas, leeks, lettuce,
   tomatoes, squash, and start the potato chitting.
8. Plant potatoes at the frost-free date (mid-April in most of Central Europe);
   earth up as they grow.
9. Direct sow: carrots, beetroot, peas, beans (after last frost), radish.
10. Mesh over the brassicas and carrots **on day one**, not after the damage.

**Summer**
11. Water deeply, mulch heavily, weed weekly (little and often — an hour a week
    beats a day a month).
12. **Start saving seed** from beans, peas, lettuce and tomatoes.
13. Preserve as you go — don't wait for a glut you can't process.
14. Sow the autumn/winter crops in July–August: kale, chard, leeks, spring
    cabbage, winter salad, purple sprouting broccoli.

**Autumn**
15. Harvest, cure and store: potatoes (dry in the dark for 2 weeks first),
    onions, squash (cure 10 days in sun), roots into sand.
16. Sow green manure on every empty bed.
17. **Write down what worked and what failed.** Plan year two from your own data.
18. Add two more beds.

## 13. Garden checklist

```
[ ] Usable growing area measured, sun-mapped and written down
[ ] Soil pH tested
[ ] First beds built (no-dig) and planted, whatever the season
[ ] 4-year rotation plan drawn on paper
[ ] Calorie crops are 50%+ of the planned area, not just salad
[ ] Open-pollinated seed bank for 3–5 years bought and stored cold and dry
[ ] Seed germination test done this year
[ ] Seed saved from at least beans, peas, tomatoes and lettuce
[ ] Seed potatoes and garlic for next year set aside
[ ] Compost bays built and filling
[ ] Urine diverted and used as fertiliser
[ ] Green manure sown on every bare bed
[ ] Comfrey patch planted
[ ] Lime, rock dust and bone meal stocked
[ ] Mesh, netting and fleece bought — enough to cover everything
[ ] Slug strategy in place before spring
[ ] Blight-resistant potato varieties chosen
[ ] Fruit trees, bushes, rhubarb and asparagus PLANTED (this is the urgent one)
[ ] Nut trees planted
[ ] Perennial vegetables and herbs established
[ ] Rainwater irrigation from tanks, with mulch everywhere
[ ] Cold frame or polytunnel up; spare polythene in store
[ ] Full set of quality hand tools + spares + sharpening kit
[ ] Root cellar or cool store ready before the first harvest
[ ] Preservation equipment ready BEFORE the glut (jars, canner, dehydrator)
[ ] Hungry-gap crops (kale, leeks, parsnips, PSB, overwintered beans) planted
[ ] Garden notebook started
```
