# 10 — Security, Community & Communications

*The fortress mentality is the most seductive and most wrong idea in
preparedness. Isolated households fail. Connected neighbourhoods endure. This
chapter is about being un-noticed, being well-connected, and being informed.*

---

## 1. The realistic threat picture

In a pandemic, the security risks are — in order of actual likelihood:

1. **Opportunistic theft** — an unlocked shed, a bicycle, a generator left out, a
   delivery on a doorstep, fuel siphoned from a car. Common, low-drama, and
   almost entirely preventable with locks and habits.
2. **Desperate neighbours** — people who did not prepare, in week six, who know
   or suspect you did. This is a **social** problem, not a tactical one, and it
   is by far the most likely difficulty you will face.
3. **Burglary of empty or apparently empty properties** — rises in any economic
   crisis.
4. **Disputes** — over water, fuel, boundaries, noise, animals, or somebody's
   behaviour during a stressful confinement. More likely to harm you than any
   stranger.
5. **Fire and accident** with no emergency services available.
6. **Organised violence** — very unlikely in a pandemic scenario in a stable
   European country, and if it does occur, no household plan defends against it.
   Community, distance and leaving are the only real answers.

**Calibrate your effort accordingly.** Good locks, good lighting discipline, good
neighbours and a fire extinguisher will do more for your safety than anything
tactical. Preparing for scenario 6 while neglecting scenarios 1–5 is a
misallocation that many people make.

## 2. OPSEC — the discipline of not being interesting

**The best security is that nobody thinks you have anything.** Everything below
is free.

**Before the crisis**
- **Don't talk about your preparations.** Not at work, not at parties, not on
  social media, not in a Facebook group, not "just to a friend". Every person who
  knows is a person who will remember your address in week six — and who will
  mention it to someone else.
- Take deliveries discreetly; break down and dispose of packaging that advertises
  (a stack of 20 L bucket boxes at the kerb is a billboard).
- Keep the shed, garage and greenhouse locked, and the good tools out of sight.
- Be careful with photos: a picture of your pantry, your solar array or your
  chickens, geotagged, is an inventory with a map.

**During the crisis**
- **Light discipline.** In a dark street, a brightly lit window is visible for a
  kilometre. Blackout curtains or blankets over windows after dark. Use low,
  warm, directed light — headlamps rather than room lights.
- **Sound discipline.** A generator is audible for hundreds of metres and says
  "this house has fuel and things worth powering". Run it in daylight, when other
  noise exists, for short batch-charging sessions (Ch. 02), inside a baffle box
  or behind a wall, and never at night.
- **Smell discipline.** Cooking smells carry astonishingly far when nobody else
  is cooking. Cook at conventional mealtimes; use enclosed stoves; be careful
  with baking bread and roasting meat when others are hungry.
- **Sight discipline.** Don't unload supplies in view. Don't let a visible garden
  full of food face a public road without a hedge or a fence. Keep the front of
  the house looking as unremarkable and as occupied-but-ordinary as everyone
  else's.
- **Routine discipline.** Vary times if you must go out. Don't establish a visible
  pattern.
- **Conversation discipline.** "We're managing, same as everyone" is the answer.
  Not "we're fine, we've got a year of food".

## 3. Layered physical security — in order of value

```
1. RELATIONSHIPS  — neighbours who look out for you   (free, best)
2. APPEARANCE     — nothing worth taking, nothing unusual  (free)
3. DETERRENCE     — lighting, dogs, visible locks, a lived-in look  (cheap)
4. BARRIERS       — doors, locks, windows, fences, gates  (moderate)
5. DETECTION      — alarms, sensors, a dog, a watch rota  (moderate)
6. DELAY & REFUGE — a securable inner room, a plan, a way out  (last)
```

**Practical, legal, and worth doing:**
- **Upgrade the doors and locks.** Most break-ins are the front or back door
  forced in seconds. A solid door, a good multi-point lock or a deadbolt with a
  long strike screwed into the frame studs, hinge bolts, and a door chain cost a
  few hundred euros and are the single most effective physical measure.
- **Window locks** and laminated security film on accessible glass (film keeps a
  broken window in one piece — cheap and very effective).
- **Motion-sensor lights** (solar ones need no wiring and work in a blackout).
- **A dog.** An ordinary, noisy family dog is the best intruder detection there
  is, and good company through a long confinement. Factor in a year of dog food.
- **Fences, gates and a thorny hedge** — hawthorn, blackthorn, berberis, rugosa
  rose. A thorn hedge is a fence that grows itself, feeds birds, gives you fruit,
  and looks like gardening rather than fortification.
- **Secure the outbuildings** — this is where most theft actually happens.
- **Don't store all your supplies in one place.** Split the store across at least
  two locations (cellar + loft + a neighbour's garage). A single loss should never
  be total.
- **A refuge room** — one room with a solid door and a lock where the family can
  gather, with phones, light and water, while you wait for a situation to pass or
  for help. This is the cheap, sane version of a "safe room".

**On firearms:** laws vary enormously by country and this plan makes no
recommendation. If you already own firearms lawfully, secure storage, safe
handling, training and ammunition rotation are your responsibility. If you do
not, a crisis is the worst possible time to acquire and learn. **Far more
households are harmed by a badly stored weapon than are saved by one**, and no
weapon solves the problems in §1.

## 4. Community — the actual survival strategy

**Build a mutual-aid group of 3–8 households. Start this month.**

One household cannot: keep a 24-hour watch, contain a fire, treat a serious
injury, cover every skill, share the cost of a well or a generator, care for
children while an adult is ill, or look after two people when both adults are
sick at once. **A group of five households can do all of that.**

### How to build one, starting from nothing
1. **Start with something ordinary.** A street WhatsApp/Signal group. A tool
   library. A bulk-buy order. A shared skip. A seed swap. Helping an elderly
   neighbour with shopping. None of this requires mentioning "prepping" at all.
2. **Notice who is capable and who is reliable.** Over a few months you will
   learn who shows up. Those are your people.
3. **Have the conversation gently**, one household at a time, framed around
   things everyone remembers: "after the last few years, I've been thinking we
   should have a plan as a street for if the power goes out for a week."
4. **Agree small, concrete things:** a check-in signal, who has a chainsaw, who
   is medically trained, who has a generator, who lives alone and needs checking
   on, where you'd meet.
5. **Grow it into:** shared bulk purchasing (cheaper for everyone, less
   conspicuous than one household buying a tonne of rice), shared expensive kit
   (a chainsaw, a log splitter, a pressure canner, a generator, a freezer), a skill
   exchange, childcare, and a watch rota if it ever comes to that.

### What a good group has
- **Complementary skills:** medical (a nurse or paramedic is worth more than any
  amount of kit), electrical, mechanical, building, growing, animal husbandry,
  cooking and preserving, childcare, and at least one person everyone trusts to
  mediate an argument.
- **Complementary resources:** someone with land, someone with a well, someone
  with a workshop, someone with a vehicle, someone with a big freezer.
- **Agreed decision-making.** How do you decide something, and who decides when
  you disagree? Sort this out while it is theoretical.
- **Explicit reciprocity.** Generosity works between people who both give. Agree
  what is shared, what is lent, and what is private — before scarcity makes it
  emotional.

### The rural/urban link
If you are in a flat, **cultivate a rural contact**: a relative, a friend with
land, an allotment, a farm you buy from directly. If you have land, cultivate
urban contacts who have skills, tools and hands. This link runs both ways in a
crisis and both sides need it.

## 5. The charity plan — decide this now, in calm

**In week six, someone you know will knock on your door and say their children
are hungry.** Decide today what you will do, because deciding in the moment, at
the door, with your own children behind you, is how people end up making choices
they cannot live with.

**The reasoning:**
- Turning everyone away hardens you, poisons your relationships with the
  community you depend on, and — practically — creates the exact resentment that
  makes you a target.
- Giving without limit empties your stores and helps nobody, including the person
  you gave to.
- **So set a policy, in advance, that you can sustain and explain.**

**A workable approach:**
1. **Set aside a "charity shelf" as a deliberate line item** — perhaps 10% of
   your store, bought and packed separately. Cheap, bulky, high-calorie items:
   rice, oats, flour, beans, pasta, oil, salt. This is not "spare" food; it is
   budgeted, and giving it does not touch your family's rations.
2. **Give at the gate, not in the house.** Nobody needs to see inside.
3. **Give food, not information.** Never confirm how much you have.
4. **Prefer giving through the group or a community point** — a church, a
   neighbour who is organising, a street food table. It is more effective, more
   dignified for the recipient, and it does not mark your door.
5. **Prefer giving work** where you can: "I can't give much, but there's a day's
   work here and you'll eat with us." Dignity matters, and labour is a thing you
   will need.
6. **Prioritise:** children, the elderly, the ill, and the people in your
   mutual-aid group — in that order.
7. **Have a line, and hold it kindly.** "This is what we can give. I'm sorry it
   isn't more." No lectures, no justifications, no arguments at the door.
8. **Help people prepare NOW instead.** Every neighbour who has two weeks of food
   is one who will not knock in week three. **Sharing this plan is a security
   measure**, and the cheapest one available.

## 6. Communications

### Getting information in
- **Battery/wind-up/solar radio** with **FM + AM + shortwave**. €40–80.
  FM gives you local and national broadcasting; **shortwave gives you the
  international picture when domestic media is disrupted or unreliable**. Buy one
  now, and learn to use it — listening to shortwave is a skill.
- **A second radio** in the go-bag. Radios are cheap; being uninformed is not.
- **DAB** dies with the transmitter network's power; **FM and AM are far more
  robust**. Don't rely on DAB alone.
- **NOAA/weather band** or your national weather service — weather matters a lot
  more when your food is outdoors and your heat is wood.
- Note official emergency broadcast frequencies for your country **on paper**.

### Talking to each other
- **Mobile networks fail** — cells have 2–8 hours of battery, and networks
  congest instantly in a crisis. **SMS often gets through when calls do not.**
- **PMR446 / FRS radios** (licence-free in the EU): €50 a pair, 1–3 km in real
  terrain. Perfect for the street, the garden, a shopping trip, and the
  mutual-aid group. Buy 4, plus rechargeable batteries.
- **Amateur radio (ham)** is the serious option: real range, repeater networks,
  emergency traffic handling, and a community of skilled operators. It requires a
  licence (a few weeks of study and an exam — do it now, it's genuinely
  interesting) and €50–200 for a handheld. **A licensed operator in your
  mutual-aid group is a major asset.**
- ⚠️ **Transmitting on amateur frequencies without a licence is illegal**, and
  "it's an emergency" only covers genuine immediate danger to life. Get licensed
  in GREEN.
- **Agreed radio schedule:** the group checks in at a fixed time on a fixed
  channel, e.g. 09:00 and 19:00 daily. A schedule saves batteries and means a
  missed check-in is itself information.
- **Low-tech signals**, agreed in advance: a coloured cloth in a window (green =
  all well, red = help needed), a whistle code, a torch signal at night, a
  chalk mark. Free, silent, and they work when everything else doesn't.

### The family contact plan (write it, print it, everyone carries a copy)
```
- Out-of-area contact person (often reachable when local networks are congested)
- Everyone's number, address, workplace, school — ON PAPER
  (nobody memorises numbers any more; a dead phone is total memory loss)
- Primary rally point (home) and secondary rally point (if home is unreachable)
- Check-in times and the order to try: SMS → call → radio → in person
- What each person does if separated, per scenario, per location
- Where the spare key, the documents, the cash and the go-bag are
- Medical information for each person
```
**Practise it with the children.** Twice a year.

### Offline information — build a paper library
The internet will not always be there, and a phone with a dead battery contains
nothing. **Print what matters.**
- Medical: *Where There Is No Doctor*, *Where There Is No Dentist*, a first aid
  manual
- Food: a preserving book with **tested** canning times, a bread book, a
  fermentation book, a foraging guide **with good photographs** for your region
- Growing: a vegetable growing guide for your climate, a seed-saving guide, a
  fruit pruning guide
- Practical: basic electrics and plumbing, engine and bicycle maintenance,
  knots, sewing, soap making, butchery
- Local: paper maps of your region (road and topographic), a compass
- **Your own plan**, printed, in a binder, with the checklists filled in
- An **e-reader** with thousands of books uses about 1 Wh per day and is an
  outstanding use of a small solar system — but keep the critical references on
  paper as well. Electronics fail; paper doesn't.

## 7. Security & communications checklist

```
[ ] Nobody outside the household knows the scale of our preparations
[ ] No photos, posts or geotagged evidence of supplies exist
[ ] Blackout curtains or blankets for every window
[ ] Generator siting, timing and baffling planned for noise
[ ] Doors and locks upgraded; hinge bolts and long strike plates fitted
[ ] Window locks; security film on accessible glass
[ ] Outbuildings and tools secured
[ ] Motion-sensor (solar) lighting fitted
[ ] Supplies split across at least two locations
[ ] Refuge room identified
[ ] Fire extinguishers, blanket and smoke alarms — no fire brigade coming
[ ] 3–8 household mutual-aid group forming, with names and numbers
[ ] Group skills mapped; gaps identified
[ ] Shared kit and bulk-buying arrangements agreed
[ ] Charity shelf bought, packed separately, and its policy agreed in writing
[ ] Neighbours encouraged and helped to prepare themselves
[ ] FM/AM/shortwave radio + spare, with batteries
[ ] PMR446 radios ×4 with rechargeables; ham licence being pursued
[ ] Radio check-in schedule agreed with the group
[ ] Low-tech signals agreed (window cloth, whistle, torch)
[ ] Family contact plan written, printed, carried, and practised
[ ] Paper library printed and shelved
[ ] Paper maps and a compass
[ ] Rural↔urban contact established
```
