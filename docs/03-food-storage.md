# 03 — Food Storage

*Stored food is the cheapest, fastest, most certain preparation you can make.
It is also the one people get most wrong — by storing the wrong things, in the
wrong way, in the wrong quantities, and then never eating any of it.*

---

## 1. The calorie maths

Everything starts here. Not "cans" or "boxes" — **kilocalories**.

| Person | kcal/day sedentary | kcal/day with manual work |
|---|---|---|
| Adult man | 2,400 | 3,200 – 4,000 |
| Adult woman | 1,900 | 2,600 – 3,200 |
| Teenager | 2,200 – 2,800 | 3,000 – 3,600 |
| Child 4–10 | 1,400 – 1,900 | — |
| Toddler | 1,000 – 1,300 | — |
| Pregnant / breastfeeding | +300 / +500 | — |

**Crisis life is manual work.** Hauling water, chopping wood, digging, hand
washing, walking instead of driving — and in a cold house you burn several
hundred extra kcal a day just staying warm. **Plan on 2,800–3,000 kcal/day for
working adults.** Undereating is not a minor discomfort: it destroys your
ability to do the physical work the situation demands, wrecks your immune
system exactly when infection is the threat, and sinks morale.

### Your household's requirement

```
Adults ___ × 2,800 kcal   = __________
Others (from table)       = __________
                            ----------
            TOTAL / day   = __________ kcal

× 90 days   (L2) = __________ kcal
× 365 days  (L3) = __________ kcal
```

**Family of four (2 adults, 2 children): ≈ 9,000 kcal/day → 810,000 kcal for
3 months → 3,285,000 kcal for a year.**

Converting to weight: staple dry goods average roughly **3,400 kcal/kg**
(grains 3,400 · pasta 3,600 · beans 3,400 · sugar 4,000 · oil 8,800 · rice 3,600).

```
3,285,000 kcal ÷ 3,400 kcal/kg ≈ 965 kg of dry staples for a family of four per year
```

That is about **1 tonne of food**, occupying roughly **1.5–2 m³** — a stack of
about 50 × 20 L buckets, or one corner of a cellar, garage or spare room
(2 m × 1 m × 1 m). It is far less space than people imagine, and at bulk prices
costs roughly **€900–1,600**. That is the single best money in this entire plan.

## 2. The one-year staple base (per adult)

This is the classic, proven long-term storage baseline, adapted metric. It
delivers roughly **2,300–2,600 kcal/day** with adequate protein. Multiply by
your household; scale children at 0.6–0.8.

| Category | kg/adult/year | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Grains** | **180 kg** | Wheat berries, rice, oats, maize, barley, pasta, flour |
| **Legumes** | **30 kg** | Dry beans, lentils, chickpeas, split peas, soy |
| **Fats & oils** | **12 kg (≈13 L)** | Olive oil, sunflower oil, coconut oil, ghee, lard, tallow |
| **Sugars** | **20 kg** | Sugar, honey, jam, syrup, dried fruit |
| **Dairy** | **8 kg** | Whole/skim milk powder, hard cheese, UHT, butter powder |
| **Salt** | **4 kg** | Iodised table + coarse/pickling salt for preserving |
| **Protein extras** | **15–25 kg** | Tinned fish/meat, dried eggs, TVP, nuts, seeds, jerky |
| **Vegetables & fruit** | **40–60 kg** | Tinned, dried, freeze-dried, plus your own preserves |
| **Extras** | as needed | Yeast, baking powder, bicarbonate, vinegar, spices, tea, coffee, bouillon, cocoa |

**For a family of four, one year: ~720 kg grain, 120 kg legumes, 50 L oil,
80 kg sugar, 32 kg dairy powder, 16 kg salt.**

### Do not build this as a monolith
Split your storage into **three layers**, because they serve different purposes:

**Layer A — Everyday deep pantry (2–8 weeks).** Ordinary food you eat weekly,
just more of it. Tins, pasta, rice, coffee, oil, UHT milk, frozen goods. Rotates
naturally. **Build this first — it covers 90% of realistic events.**

**Layer B — Bulk staples (3–12 months).** Sacks of grain, beans, sugar, salt and
oil, packed for the long term. Cheap per calorie, boring, requires cooking skill
and fuel. This is your insurance layer.

**Layer C — Morale and micronutrients.** Coffee, tea, chocolate, spices,
alcohol, sweets, sauces, vitamins, sprouting seed. **Weighs almost nothing,
costs little, and is worth far more than its calories.** Month four of lentils
with no seasoning breaks people. This layer is not a luxury; it is what makes
Layer B edible for a year.

## 3. Shelf life — what actually lasts

### Effectively indefinite (25–30+ years, packed properly)
White rice · wheat berries · maize/corn · rolled oats · dry beans (they get
harder — cook with a pressure cooker) · lentils · pasta · sugar · **salt (forever)**
· **honey (forever)** · milk powder · dried potato flakes · baking soda ·
freeze-dried food.

### Long (2–10 years)
Tinned food (long past the date if the tin is sound — see below) · white flour
(1–2 yrs; wheat berries are far better) · dried fruit · nuts in the shell ·
vinegar · spirits · bouillon · vitamins (2–3 yrs) · TVP.

### Short — rotate hard (6 months – 2 years)
- **Brown rice: 6–12 months.** The bran oil goes rancid. Store white rice for the
  long term; brown rice only in your rotating pantry.
- **Wholemeal flour: 3–6 months.** Store the whole grain and mill it as needed.
- **Cooking oils: 1–2 years.** Olive and coconut keep best; sunflower and rapeseed
  go rancid faster. Store cool and dark, buy in tins or dark glass, and rotate
  relentlessly. **Fat is both the hardest thing to store and the hardest thing to
  produce.** Consider ghee (2 yrs at room temperature) and rendered lard/tallow
  (1 yr cool, longer canned or frozen).
- Nuts and seeds shelled: 6–12 months (freeze to extend).
- Coffee beans: 1–2 years sealed; ground, much less. Green (unroasted) coffee
  beans keep 5+ years — buy green and roast in a pan.
- **Anything with fat in it is on a clock.** This is the rule that governs food
  storage.

### About "best before" dates
On shelf-stable food these are quality dates, not safety dates. Tinned goods
stored cool are usually good for 5–10 years and often far longer; quality and
vitamin content decline, safety usually does not. **Exceptions — throw away
without tasting:** any tin that is bulging, leaking, rusted through, or spurts
when opened; anything that smells or looks wrong; any home preserve with a
failed seal. Botulism has no taste and no smell. High-acid tinned foods
(tomatoes, fruit, anything vinegared) degrade fastest and can corrode the tin
from inside — rotate those on the printed date.

## 4. How to pack for 25 years

The enemies are **oxygen, moisture, light, heat, and pests**. Remove all five.

**The standard method — the only one worth learning:**

```
Dry staple  →  Mylar bag (≥ 5 mil / 100 µm)  →  O₂ absorber  →  heat-seal
            →  food-grade bucket (20 L) with gasket lid  →  cool, dark, dry
```

1. **Mylar bags**, 5–7 mil, 20 L size (typically 50 × 60 cm). €1–2 each. Mylar is
   an oxygen and light barrier; plastic buckets alone are not (they breathe).
2. **Oxygen absorbers.** Sizing: **2,000 cc per 20 L bucket** (or 2 × 1,000 cc);
   300–500 cc for a 4 L bag. Work fast — once the packet is open, absorbers are
   live; keep unused ones in a sealed jar with the air squeezed out. A correctly
   sealed bag goes hard and "bricked" within a day — that is your proof it worked.
3. **Seal with a hair straightener, clothes iron on a board, or an impulse
   sealer** (€30). Leave one corner open, press out the air, seal the last corner.
4. **Food-grade bucket** with a gamma-seal or gasket lid. The bucket is pest and
   crush protection; the mylar does the real work.
5. **Moisture must be < 10%** going in, or you create a botulism risk in an
   anaerobic bag. Shop-dry grains and beans are fine. **Never** pack anything you
   dried yourself unless it is cracker-dry, and never pack anything with fat
   (nuts, brown rice, ground flour) this way for the long term.
6. **Label the outside AND put a label inside:** contents, weight, date packed,
   date to review. You will not remember. Use permanent marker and tape; ink fades.

**Alternatives:** food-grade 200 L barrels with a bag liner for grain in bulk;
glass jars with O₂ absorbers for small quantities (rodent-proof and reusable —
excellent for apartments); vacuum sealing (good for 1–5 years, but the bags are
oxygen-permeable over decades and puncture easily).

**Pest control:** weevil eggs are already in most grain when you buy it.
Freezing a sack for **72 hours at −18 °C** kills eggs and larvae — do this before
packing. Diatomaceous earth (food grade) mixed at ~1% also works and is harmless.
O₂-absorbed mylar kills insects anyway by suffocation, which is a large part of
why the method works.

**Storage conditions.** Cool is everything: **shelf life roughly halves for every
+10 °C.** Aim for 10–18 °C, dark, dry, off the floor on pallets or boards,
away from chemicals and fuel. A hot loft is the worst place in the house; a
cellar or a north-facing interior room is the best.

## 5. The nutrition trap

A year of grain, beans, sugar and oil will keep you alive and will slowly make
you ill. Long-term storage diets are reliably deficient in:

| Nutrient | Deficiency | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| **Vitamin C** | **Scurvy in 8–12 weeks.** Bleeding gums, wounds that won't heal, exhaustion, then death. | **Sprouts** (Ch. 09) · sauerkraut and other lacto-ferments · rosehips · dried/tinned fruit · **vitamin C tablets (dirt cheap, buy 3 years' worth — this is the single best-value item in your store)** |
| **Vitamin A** | Night blindness, immune failure | Carrots (store well), squash, dried greens, liver, cod liver oil, multivitamin |
| **Vitamin D** | Immune and bone problems — **and near-universal in Central European winters even in normal times** | Supplement (everyone, every winter), oily fish, eggs, sunlight |
| **B12** | Neurological damage, anaemia | Tinned fish/meat, eggs, dairy powder, fortified yeast, supplement |
| **Folate, B-complex** | Anaemia, fatigue | Sprouts, legumes, greens, supplement |
| **Omega-3** | Inflammation, mood, cognition | Tinned oily fish (sardines/mackerel — cheap, long shelf life, outstanding value), linseed/flax, walnuts |
| **Iron & zinc** | Anaemia, poor immunity, slow healing | Meat, legumes + vitamin C to aid absorption, cast-iron cooking |
| **Fibre & gut health** | Constipation, misery | Whole grains, ferments, vegetables |
| **Fat** | Energy collapse, vitamin A/D/E/K absorption failure | Oils, nuts, fatty meat. **Do not build a low-fat store.** |
| **Complete protein** | Muscle loss, poor healing | Combine grains + legumes at the same meal (rice + beans, bread + lentils) — together they are a complete protein |

**The three cheap interventions that fix most of this:**
1. **A jar of sprouting seed** (mung, lentil, alfalfa, radish, broccoli, fenugreek)
   — 5 kg costs €25, stores for years, and gives fresh vitamin-C-rich food in
   **3–5 days, indoors, with no light, soil or power, in any season.** It is the
   single most important nutritional item in the whole plan.
2. **A multivitamin + vitamin C + vitamin D per person per day for a year.**
   ~€40/person/year. Rotate every 2–3 years.
3. **Cabbage and salt.** A 5 kg crock of sauerkraut needs nothing but cabbage,
   salt and time, keeps for months, and is a serious source of vitamin C
   and gut flora.

**Special diets need special planning:** infants (formula has a hard expiry and no
substitute — plan explicitly and generously), coeliac, diabetic (a high-carb
storage diet is dangerous — build around legumes, fats and proteins instead),
allergies. These cannot be improvised and must be solved in GREEN.

## 6. Preservation — turning harvests into stores

This is the skill set that converts "I grew food" into "I have food in February".

### Lacto-fermentation — start here
Easiest, cheapest, needs no power, no equipment beyond a jar, and *adds*
nutrition (vitamin C, B vitamins, probiotics).
- **Brine: 2–3% salt by weight** (20–30 g salt per litre of water, or per kg of
  vegetables for dry-salted kraut).
- Keep everything under the brine — exposure to air is what spoils it.
- Ferment at 18–22 °C for 3–14 days, then move somewhere cold (cellar, 2–10 °C)
  where it keeps for **6–12 months**.
- Works on: cabbage (sauerkraut), cucumbers, carrots, beetroot, turnips, beans,
  peppers, garlic, mixed vegetables, hot sauce.
- **Buy salt in bulk.** It never expires, it is essential to almost every
  preservation method, and it is one of the few things you genuinely cannot
  produce at home inland.

### Water-bath canning — high-acid foods only
For **pH ≤ 4.6**: fruit, jams, pickles, tomatoes *with added acid*, chutneys,
anything vinegared. Jars are submerged in boiling water for a set time.
Equipment: a big pot with a rack, jars, and **new lids every time** (screw bands
are reusable, sealing lids are not — stock several hundred, or buy the European
style with replaceable rubber rings).

### Pressure canning — the homestead game-changer
The **only safe way to preserve low-acid foods**: meat, poultry, fish, stock,
beans, and all non-acidified vegetables. It reaches 116–121 °C, which is what
destroys *Clostridium botulinum* spores. Boiling water canning cannot do this,
and the failure mode is fatal.
- A quality pressure canner (All American / Presto class) is **€200–400** and is
  arguably the highest-value single tool a homestead can own: it lets you store
  meat, stock and vegetables **without a freezer and without electricity** once
  the jars are done.
- **Follow a tested, published recipe for time and pressure. Every time.**
  (National Center for Home Food Preservation / Ball Blue Book — print them.)
  This is not a place for improvisation or old family guesswork.
- Adjust pressure for altitude.
- Buy: the canner, a spare gasket and safety plug, 200+ jars, 500 lids, a jar
  lifter, a funnel.

### Drying / dehydrating
Ancient, cheap, and space-efficient — dried food is ~20% of its wet weight.
- **Solar drier** (a glazed box with mesh trays and a vent) costs €40 in materials
  and uses no power. Ideal in a Central European August.
- **Electric dehydrator** (€60–150, ~300–500 W) is faster and weather-independent
  — run it off surplus solar at midday.
- Works on: fruit, tomatoes, mushrooms, herbs, onions, peppers, beans, courgette,
  and meat (jerky — must be lean; fat goes rancid).
- Store dried food in jars or mylar with an O₂ absorber. **It must be
  crisp-dry**, or it moulds.

### Root cellaring — zero energy, huge capacity
A cellar, a buried barrel, a clamp or an unheated north-facing pantry holding
**2–8 °C at 85–95% humidity** will keep, with no power at all:
- Potatoes (dark, 4–8 °C — **never below 4 °C or they go sweet**): 5–8 months
- Carrots, beetroot, parsnip, turnip, celeriac (in damp sand): 4–6 months
- Cabbage (hung by the root): 3–5 months · Onions & garlic (dry, cool, airy,
  0–5 °C): 6–9 months · Apples (wrapped, away from potatoes): 3–6 months ·
  Winter squash & pumpkins (dry, 10–15 °C — warmer than the rest): 4–8 months
- Keep apples separate from everything: their ethylene sprouts potatoes and
  spoils neighbours.
- A **clamp** (potatoes/roots in a heap on straw, covered with straw then 20 cm
  of soil, with a ventilation chimney) costs nothing and works.

### Salting, curing and smoking
Salt pork, bacon, ham, salt fish, biltong. Effective, needs care with nitrates for
anything cured cold and slow. Learn from a good book **before** you have a
carcass and no freezer. Simple dry-salting of pork belly and legs is reliable and
ancient; cold-smoked charcuterie is an advanced skill with a real risk.

### Fat preservation
Rendered lard and tallow keep 6–12 months in a cool dark place, longer canned.
**Confit** — meat cooked slowly in fat and stored submerged in it — keeps for
months in a cellar. Fat sealing is an excellent, half-forgotten technique.

### Freezing
Fast and easy, but **it is the least resilient method** — it depends on
uninterrupted power for its entire life. Use the freezer as your working buffer,
not your strategic store, and plan to **empty it into jars, the dehydrator and
the smoker** if power becomes unreliable. Keep a bag of ice or a frozen bottle in
the freezer with a coin on top: if you return to find the coin at the bottom, it
thawed and refroze while you were away — do not eat the contents.

### Eggs without refrigeration
Unwashed fresh eggs keep **2–4 weeks at room temperature** and **2–3 months in a
cool cellar** — the natural bloom is intact, so don't wash them until use.
**Water glassing** (submerging clean, unwashed, fresh eggs in a 1:10 solution of
pickling lime / calcium hydroxide) keeps them edible for **12–18 months** without
power. This is the classic way to carry spring's egg glut through winter.

## 7. Rotation — the discipline that makes it real

Stored food you never touch is money spent on rubbish you will throw away.

**The rules:**
1. **Eat what you store, store what you eat.** Every item in Layer A and most of
   Layer B should appear in your normal cooking.
2. **FIFO.** New goes to the back. Physically arrange shelves so this happens
   automatically (a can rotator, or simply loading from one end).
3. **Date everything** with a marker on purchase, not just the printed date.
4. **Keep a written inventory.** Use
   [`worksheets/inventory-template.csv`](../worksheets/inventory-template.csv).
   Update it when you open a bucket and when you shop. An unknown store is not a store.
5. **The quarterly "eat-the-store week"** — cook only from storage for one week
   every three months. It finds the gaps (no yeast! no oil! nobody likes the
   dried soya!) while fixing them is still trivial.
6. **Learn to cook it now.** Dry beans need soaking and 1–2 hours of fuel
   (30 minutes in a pressure cooker). Wheat berries need a mill. Powdered milk
   needs technique to taste acceptable. Sourdough needs a starter you keep alive.
   Discovering this in month two is a disaster; discovering it this Saturday is a
   pleasant afternoon.
7. **Buy in tranches, not in one panic.** A little extra every shop builds a year
   of food invisibly, without straining budget or supply.

## 8. Tools that make stored food usable

| Tool | € | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **Hand grain mill** (quality steel burr, e.g. Wondermill Junior / Komo hand) | 120–350 | Wheat berries store 30 years; flour stores 6 months. Without a mill, bulk grain is chicken feed. **Buy the hand version even if you own an electric one.** |
| Electric grain mill | 200–400 | Convenience while power lasts |
| **Pressure cooker** (6–10 L) | 60–120 | Cuts bean and grain cooking fuel by 50–70%. Pays for itself in fuel. |
| **Pressure canner** | 200–400 | The only safe way to preserve meat and vegetables long-term |
| **Thermal cooker / haybox** | 60–120 (or DIY free) | 15 min of fuel + 4 h of insulated coasting = a cooked stew. Enormous fuel savings. |
| Dehydrator or solar drier | 40–150 | Preserves a glut with no jars |
| Manual can opener ×3 | 15 | The classic single point of failure. Buy three good ones. |
| Grain/food scale | 15 | Ration control and fermentation salt ratios |
| Vacuum sealer | 60–120 | Medium-term storage, repacking |
| Impulse heat sealer | 30 | For mylar bags |
| Mortar & pestle / hand grinder | 20 | Spices, coffee, small grains |
| Large stockpot + strainers | 60 | Batch cooking, canning, rendering |

## 9. Build sequence

### 🏢 Apartment
Space is the constraint. **~1 m³ per person-year** of staples if densely packed.
- **Under every bed** (flat boxes, 20 L bags), **in the back of every wardrobe**,
  on top of kitchen units, under the sofa, in the hallway cupboard, on a shelf
  above the doors. A 60 cm × 200 cm under-bed space holds 150 kg of food.
- Favour **calorie-dense, low-volume, no-cook-needed** items: oil, nuts, pasta,
  rice, sugar, honey, powdered milk, tinned fish, peanut butter.
- Glass jars with O₂ absorbers on shelves are both storage and rodent-proofing,
  and they look like an ordinary pantry rather than a stockpile.
- **Fuel is the hidden constraint** — dry beans need an hour of gas. Store more
  quick-cooking items (pasta, couscous, bulgur, instant oats, tinned/ready foods)
  than a house would, plus a thermal cooker (Ch. 04).
- Target: L1 (2 weeks) easily; L2 (3 months) with discipline; L3 (12 months) only
  in a large flat with a cellar or storage unit.

### 🏡 Land / house
- Dedicate a cool room, cellar or insulated garage corner. Shelving, pallets,
  a thermometer/hygrometer, and rodent control (steel, not plastic, bins).
- Buy bulk direct: farm co-ops, mills, agricultural merchants, wholesale food
  suppliers. 25 kg sacks are typically **30–60% cheaper** per kilo than
  supermarket packs.
- **Build or convert a root cellar** — it multiplies your storage capacity for
  almost nothing and works forever.
- Then integrate with production: what you grow (Ch. 07) and raise (Ch. 08) tops
  up the store each year instead of draining it.

## 10. Food checklist

```
[ ] Household daily kcal requirement calculated
[ ] 2-week deep pantry of normal food in place (L1)
[ ] 3-month staple base bought and packed (L2)
[ ] Packing method understood; mylar + O₂ absorbers + buckets in stock
[ ] Grain frozen 72 h before packing (weevils)
[ ] Everything labelled and dated, inside and out
[ ] Written inventory exists and is current
[ ] Storage is cool (< 18 °C), dark, dry, off the floor, rodent-proof
[ ] Fat/oil stock present and on a rotation schedule
[ ] Salt: 4 kg per person per year, in bulk
[ ] Vitamin C, vitamin D and multivitamins for 12 months per person
[ ] Sprouting seed (5 kg+) and a sprouting jar/tray
[ ] Hand grain mill owned AND used at least once
[ ] Pressure cooker owned and used
[ ] Pressure canner owned, tested, tested recipes printed  (land/L3)
[ ] Manual can openers ×3
[ ] Special diets (infant, coeliac, diabetic) explicitly planned
[ ] Pet food included in the plan
[ ] Quarterly eat-the-store week scheduled in the calendar
[ ] Comfort layer: coffee, spices, chocolate, treats — do not skip this
```
