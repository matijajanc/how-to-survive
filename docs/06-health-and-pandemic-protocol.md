# 06 — Health & Pandemic Protocol

*This is the chapter that is specifically about a pandemic rather than about
self-sufficiency in general. It has two halves: **keeping the disease out**, and
**staying healthy when the health system cannot help you.***

> **Not medical advice.** Everything here is for planning and preparation.
> Build your medication supply *with* your doctor and pharmacist, never by
> skipping doses or obtaining prescription drugs outside the system. Get
> vaccinated. Follow official public health guidance — it is written by people
> with far more information than you have about the specific pathogen.
> **If someone is seriously ill, seek medical care.** A saturated hospital is
> still better than no hospital.

---

## 1. The biggest medical risk is not the virus

For most households, the greatest danger in a pandemic is **losing routine
healthcare**: the insulin you can't collect, the blood pressure medicine that
runs out, the appendix that bursts while ambulances are queued, the infected
cut, the dental abscess, the birth with no midwife.

**Therefore the priority order is:**

```
1. Prescription continuity   — start this TODAY, it takes months to build legally
2. Not catching it           — PPE, isolation, ventilation, decontamination
3. Treating what you can     — the medical chest and the skills
4. Knowing when to break isolation and get real help
```

---

## 2. Prescription medicines — the single most important preparation

**For anyone in your household on regular medication, this is the top priority in
this entire plan.** More important than food, water or power, because the
timeline is shorter: an insulin-dependent person without insulin has days.

**How to build a buffer legally and safely:**
- **Talk to your GP now**, in GREEN, and explain that you want a modest buffer for
  supply disruption. Many will support a 90-day prescription instead of 28 days,
  especially post-2020. Ask about an emergency/holiday supply.
- **Fill repeats as early as the system allows**, every time. A 7-day early refill
  each month builds a full extra month over the course of a year, entirely within
  the rules.
- **Never skip or halve doses to build a stockpile.** That does more harm than the
  shortage you are insuring against.
- **Rotate:** use the oldest first so the buffer is always fresh.
- **Get a written copy of every prescription** — drug name (generic, not brand),
  dose, frequency, and the condition treated. If you ever need care elsewhere,
  from anyone, this piece of paper is priceless.
- **Storage:** cool, dark, dry. **Most medicines degrade fastest in heat** — the
  bathroom cabinet is the worst place in the house. Many solid-dose drugs remain
  potent for years past their date (the US SLEP programme found ~88% of tested
  drugs still met specification 5+ years past expiry) — **but liquids,
  antibiotics in suspension, insulin, nitroglycerine, EpiPens, eye drops and
  anything refrigerated are genuine exceptions and lose potency or become unsafe.**
  Never gamble on a life-critical drug being still good.
- **Insulin specifically:** unopened vials need 2–8 °C. Plan the cold chain —
  a solar-powered fridge or 12 V cooler on the critical-load circuit (Ch. 02),
  a cool cellar, or a clay-pot/evaporative cooler as a last resort. An in-use
  vial keeps ~28 days at room temperature. This is a **Tier 1 electrical load**.
- **Oxygen concentrators, CPAP, nebulisers, powered wheelchairs:** each is a Tier 1
  load. Size the power system for them explicitly, and have a manual fallback
  where one exists.

**Also stock:** spare glasses (get an extra pair made and keep a copy of your
prescription), contact lens solution, hearing aid batteries, mobility aid spares,
and anything else whose absence disables someone.

## 3. The household medical chest

Build this over several months. Quantities are for a family of four for
approximately 6–12 months.

### Life-saving basics
| Item | Qty | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Oral rehydration salts (ORS)** | **40+ sachets** | **The most life-saving item on this list.** Diarrhoea and fever kill by dehydration. Home recipe: 1 L clean water + 6 tsp sugar + ½ tsp salt. |
| Digital thermometer (+ spare) | 2 | Fever monitoring |
| **Pulse oximeter** | 2 | Detects the "silent hypoxia" of respiratory illness. €20. Genuinely useful for deciding when someone needs hospital care. |
| Blood pressure monitor | 1 | For anyone with cardiovascular risk |
| Blood glucose meter + strips | if diabetic | Strips expire — rotate |
| First aid manual (printed) | 1 | Paper works in the dark |

### Pain, fever and everyday medicine
Paracetamol/acetaminophen (adult + children's liquid) · ibuprofen (adult +
children's) · aspirin (also for suspected heart attack — know the protocol) ·
antihistamine, non-drowsy and drowsy · loperamide (use with caution — not for
bloody or feverish diarrhoea; rehydration is the treatment) · oral rehydration
salts · antacids/omeprazole · laxative and stool softener · anti-emetic ·
cough suppressant and expectorant · throat lozenges · decongestant ·
hydrocortisone 1% cream · antifungal cream (athlete's foot, thrush) ·
antiseptic cream · burn gel · oral gel (toothache) · saline nasal spray ·
eye wash / saline pods · **electrolyte drinks** · vitamin C, vitamin D,
multivitamin, zinc.

### Wound care
Nitrile gloves (500) · sterile saline (or make it: 9 g salt per litre of boiled
water) · povidone-iodine and/or chlorhexidine · sterile gauze in several sizes ·
non-adherent dressings · adhesive dressings, assorted · **wound closure strips
(steri-strips) and skin glue** · micropore and cloth tape · crepe and elastic
bandages · **a trauma dressing and a CAT tourniquet** (know how to use it) ·
triangular bandages · burn dressings · blister plasters · tweezers, scissors,
a scalpel or sterile blades · a splint (SAM splint) · a thermometer cover ·
instant cold packs · safety pins · a proper first aid bag that is findable in
the dark.

### Pandemic-specific PPE
| Item | Qty | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **FFP2/FFP3 (N95/N99) respirators** | **100+** | The workhorse. **Fit matters more than rating** — a leaking FFP3 is worse than a well-fitted FFP2. Try several shapes now and find one that seals on each adult's face. Beards break the seal. |
| Surgical masks | 200 | For a sick person to wear (source control), and for lower-risk tasks |
| Nitrile gloves | 500 | |
| Safety goggles or a face shield | 1 per adult | Eyes are a transmission route |
| Disposable aprons / gowns | 50 | Or a dedicated washable "care coat" |
| Hand sanitiser (≥ 60% alcohol) | 3 L | |
| Disinfectant (bleach) | 10 L | See the dilution table in Ch. 05 §7 |
| **CO₂ meter** | 1 (€80) | The best cheap proxy for ventilation. Below 800 ppm = well ventilated. Above 1,500 ppm = the air you are breathing has been in other people's lungs. Invaluable for deciding when a room is safe. |
| **HEPA air cleaner or a DIY Corsi–Rosenthal box** | 1–2 | A box fan + 4 MERV-13/F7 filters taped into a cube costs ~€60 and outperforms many commercial units. Runs on ~50 W. Genuinely effective at reducing airborne virus indoors. |

### Skills and references (worth more than the kit)
- **Do a proper first aid course now.** Ideally a 2-day one. Then a refresher.
- Print and keep on paper: *Where There Is No Doctor* and *Where There Is No
  Dentist* (both freely available from Hesperian) — they are written precisely
  for "care when there is no professional available".
- Learn: recovery position · CPR · choking · bleeding control and tourniquet
  use · splinting · recognising dehydration, sepsis and hypoxia · fever
  management in children · wound cleaning and closure · when NOT to close a wound.

### Dental — the badly neglected one
Toothache with no dentist is one of the most miserable and morale-destroying
things that can happen in a long crisis, and a spreading dental infection can be
dangerous.
- **Get all outstanding dental work done in GREEN.** This is free preparation.
- Stock: temporary filling material (Cavit/zinc oxide, €10), clove oil (eugenol,
  a genuine topical anaesthetic), dental wax, floss, extra toothbrushes,
  bicarbonate of soda, a dental mirror and probe, and chlorhexidine mouthwash.
- Brush and floss religiously — in a high-sugar storage diet with no dentist,
  oral hygiene is preventive medicine.

### Antibiotics — a realistic note
Do not obtain or self-prescribe antibiotics. Misuse causes resistance, masks
serious illness, and the wrong drug for the wrong infection is useless or
harmful. What you *can* do: talk to your doctor about whether a standby course is
appropriate for anyone with a specific recurrent condition (some people with
COPD, recurrent UTIs, or who travel are legitimately prescribed rescue packs);
make sure any existing rescue medication (asthma inhalers, steroid packs,
EpiPens) is in date and duplicated; and focus your effort on the **prevention**
side — wound hygiene, clean water, and handwashing — which is where a household
can genuinely reduce its need for antibiotics.

## 4. Keeping the disease out — the household protocol

### The layers
No single measure works. Stack them, and accept that each is imperfect:
```
Distance → Ventilation → Filtration → Masks → Hygiene → Quarantine → Isolation
```

### Reduce contacts to the minimum
The most effective control is the number of people whose air you share. At
ORANGE and RED: no visitors indoors, one designated shopper (if any), deliveries
left at the door, outdoor meetings only.

### Ventilation is the biggest lever for an airborne pathogen
- Open windows. Cross-ventilate for 5–10 minutes every hour rather than leaving a
  window cracked all day (less heat loss, better air exchange).
- **Use the CO₂ meter to make it concrete:** ventilate until it drops below
  800 ppm. It turns an invisible risk into a number everyone in the household can
  see and act on.
- Run a HEPA cleaner or a Corsi–Rosenthal box in the room the household shares,
  and in any sick room.
- **Ventilation fights heating.** In winter, ventilate hard and briefly, wear
  layers, and accept the fuel cost — this is a trade you should consciously make
  in favour of air.

### The decontamination threshold (set this up at ORANGE)
Create an **airlock zone** just inside your entrance — a boot tray, a chair,
hooks, a bin, a bucket of 1:100 bleach, and hand sanitiser.

**Entering the house:**
1. Shoes off in the tray, outside the line.
2. Outer layer off and hung in the airlock (or into a laundry bin).
3. Mask off last, by the straps, into a bin or a dedicated paper bag.
4. **Hands washed with soap for 20 seconds** before touching anything else.
5. Shower and change if you have been somewhere high-risk.

**Incoming goods:** the realistic evidence is that surface transmission is a
minor route for respiratory viruses compared with air — so **do not exhaust
yourself disinfecting groceries.** A proportionate protocol: leave non-urgent
deliveries in a holding area (garage, porch, balcony) for 24 hours, unpack
outdoors or in the airlock, discard outer packaging, and **wash your hands**.
That is the step that matters. Wipe down only high-touch items if you wish.

**People coming in** (a returning family member, a helper): if the pathogen's
incubation allows, quarantine in a separate room for the recommended period with
daily symptom and temperature checks, masks when sharing space, and a separate
bathroom or a disinfect-after-use rule.

## 5. If someone in the household gets ill

### Set up the sick room in advance (do it at ORANGE, not when someone is coughing)
- Choose a room with a **window that opens** and, ideally, its own bathroom or
  the bathroom nearest to it.
- Put in: a bed, a chair, a bin with a liner, a jug of water and a cup, a basin,
  a thermometer, a pulse oximeter, tissues, ORS, paracetamol, a phone or a bell,
  and an air cleaner if you have one.
- Tape a **symptom and observation chart** to the door.
- Everything that goes in stays in until it is cleaned.

### Care protocol
- **One designated carer**, ideally the youngest healthy adult at lowest risk,
  and **not** anyone in a high-risk group. Minimise the number of people exposed.
- Carer wears: FFP2/FFP3 (fitted), eye protection, gloves, apron. On leaving:
  gloves off, apron off, eye protection cleaned, mask off last, **hands washed**.
- The sick person wears a surgical mask whenever the carer is in the room, if
  they can tolerate it.
- Window open, air cleaner running, door closed.
- Separate crockery, washed hot with a little bleach in the rinse. Separate towels.
- Laundry: handle minimally, don't shake it, wash hot (60 °C if possible).
- Bin: double-bagged, tied, held 72 h before joining the household waste.
- **Everything the sick person touches stays in the room.**

### Monitor and record — twice a day, written down
```
Time · Temperature · Pulse · Breaths per minute · SpO₂ (oximeter) ·
Fluids in (mL) · Urine out (rough) · Symptoms · Medication given
```
A written chart lets you see a trend instead of guessing, and it is exactly what
a medical professional will ask for if you do get through to one.

### Supportive care that actually helps
Fluids, fluids, fluids (ORS if there is diarrhoea, vomiting or high fever) ·
rest · paracetamol/ibuprofen for fever and discomfort (follow the dosing on the
packet; watch total daily paracetamol carefully — overdose is easy and is
serious) · keeping warm but not overheated · sitting up or prone positioning if
breathing is difficult · food when they want it, calories over nutrition.

### 🚨 Seek professional help — break isolation if you must — for:
- **SpO₂ below 92%** (or a sustained drop from their baseline), or severe
  breathlessness, or breathing so fast they cannot complete a sentence
- Blue lips or face · chest pain or pressure · coughing blood
- **Confusion, unable to be roused, unresponsive**
- Signs of severe dehydration: no urine for 8–12 hours, sunken eyes, no tears
  (children), dizziness on standing, skin that stays tented when pinched
- Fever with a **non-blanching rash** (glass test), a stiff neck, or a severe
  headache with light sensitivity — possible meningitis/sepsis
- Any fever in an infant under 3 months
- Seizures · severe abdominal pain (especially lower right — appendicitis) ·
  uncontrollable bleeding · a suspected fracture or head injury
- Any symptom that frightens you and is getting worse

**An overwhelmed hospital is still vastly better than a kitchen table.** The
plan is not "never get medical care" — it is "reduce the number of times you
need it, and know exactly when you do."

## 6. Staying healthy for a year indoors

The chronic risks of a long confinement are as real as the acute ones.

- **Move every day.** Muscle and cardiovascular loss is fast and it is a
  mortality risk. Bodyweight exercise, a skipping rope (€5), stairs, garden work,
  a 20-minute daily routine that everyone does. Physical work in a garden is both
  food and fitness.
- **Get daylight.** 20–30 minutes outdoors, even overcast, for circadian rhythm,
  mood and vitamin D. A balcony or a doorstep counts. Supplement vitamin D in
  winter regardless.
- **Sleep on a schedule.** Confinement destroys sleep timing, and poor sleep
  degrades immunity, judgement and mood.
- **Eat enough, and eat vegetables.** See Ch. 03 §5 — the nutrition trap is real
  and its symptoms (fatigue, poor healing, low mood, frequent infection) will be
  blamed on stress when the cause is diet.
- **Alcohol discipline.** Consumption reliably rises under confinement and stress,
  and it damages sleep, immunity, judgement, and relationships. Decide the
  household rule now, while it is an abstract question.
- **Protect your eyes, back and hands.** Unfamiliar manual work causes injuries;
  gloves, good tools and taking it slowly prevent them. An injury when there is
  no help is a category-changing event.

### Mental health — plan for it as seriously as for calories
Months of fear, confinement, boredom, grief and enforced proximity break people
and break families. This is not a soft topic; it is the reason plans fail.

- **Structure is the medicine.** A daily routine with fixed times for waking,
  meals, work, exercise, chores and a shared evening is what holds a household
  together. See Ch. 12.
- **Everyone gets a job.** Purpose and competence are protective. Children
  especially need real responsibilities, not just entertainment.
- **Everyone gets privacy**, even if it is only an hour and a corner with
  headphones. Enforce it.
- **Ration the news.** Once or twice a day, from a fixed source, for a fixed
  time. Continuous doom-scrolling is corrosive and changes nothing.
- **Stay connected.** Scheduled calls, radio check-ins with the neighbours,
  letters. Isolation is the risk factor, not the virus.
- **Plan entertainment deliberately**: books (paper), board games, cards,
  instruments, craft supplies, seeds, puzzles, projects. A crate of these costs
  €100 and is worth more than another sack of rice.
- **Mark time.** Birthdays, festivals, a special meal every week. When days are
  identical, time becomes formless and morale collapses. Deliberate celebration
  is a survival technique.
- **Watch for:** withdrawal, not eating, not sleeping, drinking, irritability
  escalating to violence, hopelessness. Talk about it early and explicitly, agree
  now that raising it is allowed, and know your local crisis line numbers —
  write them on paper.
- **Children:** honest, age-appropriate information beats overheard fragments.
  Keep schooling going in some form. Keep play sacred.
