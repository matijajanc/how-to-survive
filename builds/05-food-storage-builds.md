# 1. Root cellar (excavated)

**1–2 weeks** · **€600+** · Skill ⬤⬤⬤ · 🏡

**The highest-return "appliance" a homestead can own.** It holds 2–8 °C
year-round on **zero energy, forever**, and stores a winter's potatoes, roots,
cabbages and apples. It does the job of a chest freezer without the freezer, the
electricity, or the dependence.

## 1.1 The four storage zones

Different crops want different conditions. A good cellar has more than one place.

| Zone | Temp | Humidity | Stores | Duration |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Cold & damp** | **0–4 °C** | 90–95% | Carrots, beetroot, parsnip, turnip, celeriac, leeks (in damp sand) | 4–6 months |
| **Cold & damp** | **4–8 °C** | 85–90% | **Potatoes** (dark!), cabbage, apples (kept separate) | 5–8 months |
| **Cold & dry** | **0–5 °C** | 60–70% | Onions, garlic, shallots (hanging, airy) | 6–9 months |
| **Cool & dry** | **10–15 °C** | 50–70% | **Winter squash and pumpkins** | 4–8 months |

⚠️ **Potatoes below 4 °C turn sweet and unpleasant** (starch converts to sugar).
⚠️ **Keep apples away from everything else** — their ethylene sprouts potatoes
and spoils neighbours. A separate bay, or a sealed box.

## 1.2 The warning nobody gives you

**Stored vegetables respire.** In a sealed or poorly ventilated cellar they
consume oxygen and produce **carbon dioxide, which is heavier than air and pools
at the bottom.** People have died walking into their own root cellars, silos and
covered pits.

```
⚠️ VENTILATE BEFORE ENTERING a cellar that has been closed up.
⚠️ Open the hatch and the vents, and wait several minutes.
⚠️ Never enter alone if the cellar is below ground and has been shut.
⚠️ If a candle will not stay lit at floor level, the air is not safe. Get out.
⚠️ Dizziness, breathlessness or headache on entering = leave IMMEDIATELY.
```

The candle test is crude but it works, and it is free. Use it. This applies to
buried barrels and covered pits just as much as to walk-in cellars.

## 1.3 The design

```
                      ┌────┐ ← EXHAUST: from the CEILING, up and out
                      │ ↑↑ │    (warm, stale, humid air leaves at the top)
    ═══════════════╤══╧════╧═══════  ground level
     ▓▓▓▓▓▓ 600 mm+ of earth cover ▓▓▓▓▓▓▓
    ┌──────────────┴─────────────────────────┐
    │                                        │  ⇓ INTAKE: pipe down to
    │  ┌───────┐  ┌───────┐  ┌───────┐       │  ⇓ 150 mm ABOVE THE FLOOR
    │  │ROOTS  │  │POTATO │  │ONIONS │       │  ⇓ (cold air falls in)
    │  │in sand│  │ dark  │  │hanging│       │  │
    │  └───────┘  └───────┘  └───────┘       │  │
    │  gravel or earth floor (humidity)      │◄─┘
    └────────────────────────────────────────┘
       2–3 m below ground where the earth sits at the local annual
       mean temperature (~10 °C in Central Europe) — winter ventilation
       then chills it down to 2–8 °C and the mass holds it there
```

**The two-pipe rule is the whole design.** One pipe **low** (intake, ending
150 mm above the floor) and one pipe **high** (exhaust, from the ceiling, rising
above ground). Cold air falls in, warm humid air rises out, and the cellar
breathes by itself with no fan. **A cellar with one vent, or with both vents at
the same height, does not work.**

## 1.4 Build

1. **Check before you dig:** the water table (a cellar that floods is worthless),
   buried services, and your local building regulations. In many places an
   excavation of this size needs consent.
2. ⚠️ **An excavation over 1.2 m deep can collapse and kill.** Batter the sides
   back at 45°, or shore them properly, or hire someone with a machine and the
   competence. **This is the most dangerous build in this folder.** Never work
   alone in a trench.
3. **Excavate** to 2–3 m depth. Deeper is more stable in temperature.
4. **Structure:** reinforced concrete, concrete block with a poured lintel, dry
   stone with a vaulted roof, or a proprietary precast chamber. The roof carries
   600 mm+ of wet earth — that is well over a tonne per square metre, so it must
   be engineered, not guessed.
5. **Waterproof the outside** and lay a French drain around the base, draining to
   daylight or a soakaway. Water ingress is the number one cellar failure.
6. **Insulate the roof** (100 mm of foam board under the earth) — the roof is
   where summer heat comes in.
7. **Earth cover:** 600 mm minimum over the roof, graded to shed water away.
8. **Fit the two pipes** (110 mm) as above, with mesh caps against rodents and
   insects, and **dampers so you can throttle them** — you close them down in
   hard frost and open them wide on cold autumn nights to chill the cellar.
9. **Floor:** gravel or bare earth. Sprinkling it with water raises the humidity,
   which is exactly what roots want. A concrete floor makes it too dry.
10. **Fit out:** timber slatted shelves (never against the wall — leave 50 mm for
    airflow), boxes of damp sand for roots, hanging nets for onions, and a
    **thermometer and hygrometer** you actually read.
11. **Door/hatch:** insulated, and in a cold climate a double door creating an
    airlock stops a blast of frost every time you open it.

## 1.5 Operating it

- **Chill it in autumn:** open both vents wide on cold nights in October and
  November to drive the temperature down before you load it. Close them on warm
  days. This is active work and it decides how well the cellar performs.
- **Load in cool weather, not warm.** Cure everything first (see §7).
- **Check weekly.** Remove anything spoiling immediately — one rotten apple
  really does take the box.
- **Never store fuel, paint or chemicals in a food cellar.**

# 2. Buried barrel root cellar

**4 h** · **€60** · Skill ⬤○○ · 🏡 **The 95%-of-the-benefit version**

If you cannot build a real cellar, build this. It stores 150 kg of roots and
takes an afternoon.

```
      ═══════╤═══════════════╤═══════  ground level
             │  ▓ straw ▓    │  ← 300 mm of straw/leaves over the lid,
        ┌────┴───────────────┴────┐     held down with a board and a stone
        │  ╔═══════════════════╗  │
        │  ║   LID (sealed,    ║  │
        │  ║    liftable)      ║  │
     ▓▓▓│  ╠═══════════════════╣  │▓▓▓
     ▓▓▓│  ║                   ║  │▓▓▓  200 L food-grade barrel,
     ▓▓▓│  ║  roots in damp    ║  │▓▓▓  buried UPRIGHT with the lid
     ▓▓▓│  ║  sand, layered    ║  │▓▓▓  100 mm proud of the soil
     ▓▓▓│  ║                   ║  │▓▓▓
     ▓▓▓│  ╚═══════════════════╝  │▓▓▓
        └─────────────────────────┘
            gravel base for drainage
```

**Build**
1. Dig a hole to take a **200 L food-grade barrel** upright, with 100 mm of the
   rim left above ground so surface water cannot run in.
2. **150 mm of gravel in the bottom of the hole** for drainage — a barrel sitting
   in water will float out and your crop will rot.
3. Drop the barrel in, backfill and firm the soil around it.
4. Drill **four 10 mm holes just under the rim**, above soil level, for a little
   air exchange. Cover them with mesh.
5. Layer produce in damp (not wet) sand, sawdust or leaves, with the barrel lid on.
6. **Pile 300 mm of straw or dry leaves over the lid** and weigh it down with a
   board and a stone. This is the insulation that stops it freezing.

**Use:** one barrel for carrots/beets/parsnips in sand, another for potatoes in
the dark. ⚠️ **The same gas warning applies** — lift the lid and let it air for a
minute before reaching in, and don't put your head inside.

**Even simpler:** an old chest freezer with a dead compressor, buried to its lid,
is an excellent insulated root cellar. So is a cast-off fridge. Drill drainage
holes in the base, and **remove any door latch** before burying anything a child
could climb into.

# 3. Root clamp

**2 h** · **€5** · Skill ⬤○○ · 🏡 **Free, ancient, and it works**

The traditional way to store a tonne of potatoes or mangels with no structure at
all.

```
                 ╱▔▔▔▔╲  ← straw "chimney" left open at the top for
                ╱ ▓▓▓▓ ╲    ventilation, capped with a tile if wet
          ┌────╱────────╲────┐
         ╱  ░░ 200 mm SOIL ░░ ╲
        ╱ ▒▒▒ 200 mm STRAW ▒▒▒ ╲
       │ ●●●●●●● ROOTS ●●●●●●●  │   Heap on a bed of straw on
       │ ●●●●●●●●●●●●●●●●●●●●   │   FREE-DRAINING ground
       └──▒▒▒ straw base ▒▒▒────┘
        ═══════════════════════════ drainage ditch dug around it
```

**Build**
1. Pick a **well-drained, slightly raised spot.** Dig a shallow drainage ditch
   around where the clamp will sit.
2. Lay **200 mm of straw** as a bed.
3. Heap the crop into a ridge or cone, up to about 1 m high. **Only sound,
   dry, unwashed, uncut produce** — one damaged root rots the clamp.
4. Cover with **200 mm of straw**, leaving a twist of straw poking out of the top
   as a ventilation chimney.
5. Cover the straw with **200 mm of soil**, firmed and smoothed to shed rain,
   leaving the chimney clear.
6. In very hard frost, add more straw over the chimney.

**Open from one end only**, take what you need, and re-seal. Works for potatoes,
carrots, beetroot, swede, turnip and **mangel-wurzel** (your winter animal fodder
— see [Chapter 08](../docs/08-animals-and-protein.md)).

# 4. Cool pantry conversion

**1 day** · **€150** · Skill ⬤⬤○ · **The no-digging option**

Convert an existing north-facing room, cupboard, porch or garage corner into a
passively cooled larder. In Central Europe this holds **5–12 °C from October to
April** — which is most of what a cellar does, for a twentieth of the work.

**Build**
1. Choose the **coolest, north-facing** space with an external wall. A cupboard
   under the stairs against a north wall is ideal.
2. **Insulate it from the house**: 50–100 mm of insulation on the walls, ceiling
   and door that face the heated part of the building, and a well-sealed insulated
   door. **You are insulating it FROM the house, not from outside** — that is the
   key inversion people get wrong.
3. **Two vents through the external wall**: one **low** (intake, 150 mm above
   floor) and one **high** (exhaust, near the ceiling), both 110 mm, both
   **mesh-screened against rodents and insects**, both with closable dampers.
4. **Leave the external wall uninsulated** — it is your cooling surface.
5. Slatted timber shelves, 50 mm clear of the walls. A cold stone or tiled floor
   and a thermometer.
6. **Operate the dampers**: open at night and in cold weather, closed in warm
   spells. Close them down in hard frost to stop things freezing.

🏢 **In a flat:** an unheated north-facing balcony cupboard or a box on a north
balcony does the same job from November to March — insulated on the inside face,
vented to outside air, mesh-screened. It is free refrigeration for half the year.

# 5. Mylar packing station

**Setup: 1 h** · **€50 for the kit** · Skill ⬤○○

Not a structure — a **process and a small jig** that turns a chaotic all-day job
into a steady production line. Packing 300 kg of staples goes much faster when
the bench is set up properly.

**The kit:** mylar bags (5–7 mil, 20 L) · oxygen absorbers (**2,000 cc per 20 L
bucket**) · food-grade buckets with gasket lids · an **impulse sealer** (€30) or
a hair straightener or a clothes iron and a board · a permanent marker and labels
· a scale · a funnel or a scoop · a jar with an airtight lid for unused absorbers.

**The jig:** cut a piece of ply or card to hold the mylar bag open and upright
inside the bucket while you fill it — a simple collar, or two clamps on a board.
Without it you need three hands.

**The production line**
```
1. FREEZE the grain/beans for 72 h at −18 °C first — kills weevil eggs, which
   are already in almost all grain when you buy it.
2. Let it return to room temperature and stay DRY. Never seal anything damp:
   moisture + no oxygen = a botulism risk. Shop-dry grain and beans are fine.
3. Line the bucket with the mylar bag. Fill.
4. Drop in the O₂ absorbers — WORK FAST, they start the moment the packet opens.
   Keep the unused ones in a sealed jar with the air squeezed out.
5. Squeeze the air out, seal across the top with the iron/sealer, leaving one
   corner open. Press the last air out and seal the corner.
6. LABEL: contents, weight, date packed, review date. Marker on tape, on the
   OUTSIDE, and a paper label INSIDE the bag too.
7. Lid on the bucket. Store cool (<18 °C), dark, dry, off the floor.
8. Check the next day: a correct seal goes hard and "bricked". That is your proof.
```

⚠️ **Never pack anything with fat in it this way for the long term** — nuts,
brown rice, ground flour, anything oily. Fat goes rancid with or without oxygen.
Those go in the rotating pantry.

# 6. Cold smoker

**1 day** · **€80** · Skill ⬤⬤○ · 🏡

> ⚠️ **Read this first.** **Cold smoking does not preserve food on its own.**
> It adds flavour and some surface antimicrobial effect, but the preservation
> comes from the **curing** that must happen first — salt, and for slow cold-
> smoked products, curing salt containing nitrite. Cold smoking uncured meat at
> 20–30 °C is an **incubator for botulism and listeria.**
>
> **Always follow a tested, published cure recipe.** Do not improvise, do not
> use family guesswork, and if you are new to this, start with **hot smoking**
> (60–80 °C, which cooks the food) or with fish and cheese rather than
> charcuterie.

```
   SMOKE CHAMBER                         FIREBOX
   ┌──────────────┐
   │  ═══ hooks ══│  ← 1000 × 600 × 600 mm     smoke travels 2–4 m and
   │   │  │  │    │     insulated ply or       COOLS on the way: it must
   │  fish/meat   │     an old fridge/cabinet  arrive UNDER 30 °C for
   │   │  │  │    │                            cold smoking
   │  ═══ racks ══│
   │              │        ╔════════════════════╗
   └──────┬───────┘        ║  smouldering       ║
          └────────────────╢  sawdust /         ║
           110 mm pipe     ║  wood chips        ║
           or a covered    ╚════════════════════╝
           trench, 2–4 m   (or a separate smoke generator / maze burner)
```

**Materials:** a plywood cabinet, an old fridge with the plastic and insulation
foam stripped out, or a purpose-built box · stainless rods and hooks · 110 mm
pipe or a covered trench · a firebox (an old steel drum or a brick box) · a
damper at the chamber top · a thermometer at food level.

**Build**
1. **Chamber:** 1000 × 600 × 600 mm, with racks and hooks, a door, and an
   adjustable vent at the top. Untreated timber, stainless or an old fridge body
   (**strip out all plastic, insulation foam and the compressor first**).
2. **Firebox** 2–4 m away and **lower** than the chamber.
3. **Connect them** with buried 110 mm pipe or a covered trench. The distance is
   what cools the smoke. Longer = cooler.
4. **Thermometer at food level.** Cold smoke **must stay under 30 °C**; over that
   you are in the danger zone without cooking.
5. **Fuel:** beech, oak, alder, apple, cherry, hickory. ❌ **Never** resinous
   softwood (pine, spruce, fir) — acrid and unpleasant — and never treated,
   painted or MDF wood.
6. A **maze or spiral sawdust burner** (€25) gives 8–10 hours of clean cold smoke
   from one filling and is far easier to control than a fire.

**Use:** cure first, per a tested recipe · dry the surface until a tacky
**pellicle** forms (smoke will not adhere to a wet surface) · smoke in cool
weather — autumn and winter are the traditional smoking seasons for exactly this
reason · keep a written log of cure weights, times and temperatures.

**For hot smoking** (60–80 °C — cooks as it smokes, far more forgiving for a
beginner) move the firebox right under the chamber, or build a simple drum smoker.

# 7. Drying and curing racks

**2 h** · **€30** · Skill ⬤○○

Most storage crops need a **curing** period before they go into the cellar. Skip
it and they rot in December.

| Crop | Cure | Then store |
|---|---|---|
| **Onions & garlic** | 2–3 weeks, warm, dry, airy, out of rain until the necks are papery | Hanging, cold and dry |
| **Winter squash & pumpkins** | **10–14 days in the sun** (or 25–30 °C indoors) to harden the skin | 10–15 °C, dry, on a shelf |
| **Potatoes** | **2 weeks in the DARK at 10–15 °C** to heal the skins | 4–8 °C, dark, damp |
| **Sweet potatoes** | 7–10 days at 27–30 °C and high humidity | 13–15 °C |
| **Beans for drying** | On the plant until the pods rattle, then indoors until the bean cracks rather than dents under a hammer | Sealed jars, after 72 h freezing |
| **Herbs** | Hung in small bunches, shade, airy, 1–2 weeks | Airtight jars, whole leaves |

**Build the rack:** a simple frame with **removable mesh trays** (food-safe
stainless, nylon or food-grade plastic — **not galvanised**, zinc migrates into
acidic food), 100 mm apart, in a shaded, airy, rain-proof place: a porch, a
carport, an open barn, a greenhouse with the doors open.

**Onion and garlic braids** hung from the rafters are the classic and they work
perfectly — plaiting the dried leaves together and hanging them where air moves.
**Squash on a slatted shelf in a spare bedroom** stores better than in any cellar.
