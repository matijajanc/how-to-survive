# 1. No-dig growing beds

**1 h per bed** · **€15 per bed** · Skill ⬤○○ · **Start here, this weekend**

No digging, no rotavating, no double-digging. Cardboard on the ground, compost on
top, plant into it. It suppresses weeds, feeds the soil biology, and after 2–3
years outperforms dug beds — while costing about a fifth of the labour. **In a
crisis, where your own calories are the fuel, labour efficiency is survival
efficiency.**

```
     1200 mm bed                450–600 mm path        1200 mm bed
  ├──────────────────┤        ├──────────────┤     ├──────────────────┤
  ░░░░░░░░░░░░░░░░░░░░        ▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒       ░░░░░░░░░░░░░░░░░░░░
  ░ 100 mm COMPOST  ░        ▒ wood chip /  ▒       ░                  ░
  ▓▓▓▓ CARDBOARD ▓▓▓▓        ▒ cardboard    ▒       ▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓
  ═══ grass / soil ═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════

  1200 mm wide so you can reach the middle from either side.
  YOU NEVER STEP ON THE BED. Compacted soil is the commonest cause of poor yields.
```

**Build**
1. **Mow or strim the grass/weeds short.** Don't remove them — they are the first
   feed for the soil life.
2. **Lay plain brown cardboard**, overlapping every joint by 150 mm so no light
   gets through. **Remove all plastic tape and staples.** Wet it thoroughly.
3. **100 mm of good compost on top** — municipal green waste compost, well-rotted
   manure, or your own. A 1.2 × 5 m bed takes about 0.6 m³.
4. **Plant straight into the compost.** Transplants go in immediately; for direct
   sowing of small seed (carrots, parsnip), wait until year two or sow into a
   drill of finer compost.
5. **Edges are optional.** Unedged beds are free, easy to reshape, and don't
   harbour slugs. Timber edges look tidy and hold the compost on a slope — use
   **untreated larch, Douglas fir, sweet chestnut or oak**, or line treated timber
   with plastic.
6. **Mulch the paths** with cardboard and wood chip. No weeding, and it becomes
   compost in place.
7. **Top up with 25–50 mm of compost every autumn.** That is the entire ongoing
   maintenance.

**Perennial weeds** (couch grass, bindweed, ground elder) need more: two layers of
cardboard plus 150 mm of compost, or a season under black plastic first. They will
still push through at the edges — keep at them.

**Persuading the perimeter:** 4 beds of 1.2 × 5 m = 24 m² of growing space, which
is the right size for a first year. Add two beds a year after that.

# 2. Three-bay compost system

**4 h** · **€0–60** · Skill ⬤○○ · **Compost is the engine of the whole garden**

```
   ┌──────────┬──────────┬──────────┐   Each bay 1000 × 1000 × 1000 mm
   │  BAY 1   │  BAY 2   │  BAY 3   │
   │ FILLING  │ TURNING  │  READY   │   Fill 1 → turn into 2 → turn into 3
   │          │          │          │   Turning is what makes it fast and hot
   │ new      │ 3 months │ finished │
   │ material │   old    │ compost  │
   └──────────┴──────────┴──────────┘
    ↑ removable front boards on all three, sliding in grooves
```

**Materials:** 9 pallets (free — **heat-treated "HT" stamped only, never MB**),
or 22 × 100 boards on 70 × 70 posts · galvanised wire or brackets · 4 lengths of
45 × 45 as front-board guides · a tarpaulin or old carpet as a cover.

**Build:** stand pallets on edge to form three adjoining 1 m cubes, wired or
screwed at the corners · line the insides with cardboard if the gaps are wide ·
fit **vertical guides at the front of each bay** so boards slide in and out as
the heap grows · leave the bays open to the soil beneath (worms and soil life
must get in) · a loose cover on top to keep rain off without sealing it.

**Run it**
- **Roughly 2:1 browns to greens by volume.** Browns = straw, dry leaves,
  cardboard, wood chip, paper, spent bedding. Greens = kitchen waste, grass
  clippings, weeds, manure, comfrey.
- **Damp as a wrung-out sponge.** Too dry and nothing happens; too wet and it
  goes anaerobic and stinks.
- **Turn it into the next bay** when it cools — every 3–6 weeks. Turning is the
  difference between finished compost in 3 months and finished compost in 18.
- **1 m³ is the minimum size for a heap to heat up.** Smaller heaps compost, just
  slowly and cold — which does not kill weed seeds or pathogens.
- **Bury kitchen waste in the middle** and cover it, or you feed the rats.
- ❌ Not in the heap: meat, dairy, cooked food (rats), dog/cat faeces, diseased
  plant material, persistent perennial weed roots, anything treated with
  aminopyralid herbicides.

**Keep the humanure system entirely separate** — see
[Sanitation builds §3](04-sanitation-builds.md#3-two-bay-humanure-compost-system).

# 3. Insect mesh cage

**2 h** · **€50** · Skill ⬤○○ · **Do this before the damage, not after**

**Physical barriers are 90% of pest control without sprays.** A mesh cage over
the brassica bed defeats cabbage white butterfly, cabbage root fly, flea beetle
and pigeons in one go, permanently, with no inputs.

```
        ╔═══════════════════════════════════╗
        ║  ░░░░░ INSECT MESH 0.6–1 mm ░░░░░ ║  ← mesh, not netting.
        ║ ░                               ░ ║    Butterfly netting stops
        ║░       brassica bed              ░║    pigeons; only fine mesh
        ║░   1200 × 5000, hoops at 1 m     ░║    stops root fly and flea
        ║░                                  ░║   beetle.
        ╚═╤═══════════════════════════════╤═╝
          └── mesh edges BURIED or weighted with boards/stones all round
              (a 5 mm gap at the bottom is an open door)
```

**Materials:** 25–32 mm MDPE water pipe or 8 mm steel rod for hoops · 500 mm
lengths of rebar or timber pegs as sockets · **insect mesh, 0.6–1 mm aperture**
(€3–5/m², buy a big roll) · clothes pegs or clips · boards or stones for the edges.

**Build:** drive rebar stakes 300 mm into the soil at 1 m intervals down both
sides of the bed, leaving 200 mm proud · slide a length of MDPE pipe over each
opposing pair to form a hoop about 700 mm high · run a ridge line of pipe or
string along the top to stop them flopping · drape the mesh over the whole thing
and **seal every edge to the ground** with boards, stones or buried soil.

**Details:** mesh is fine enough that you can water straight through it · it also
gives light frost protection and wind shelter · leave it on **from the day you
plant until the day you harvest** — one afternoon of access is all a cabbage white
needs · lift it for weeding, and check for anything that got trapped inside.

**Buy plenty of mesh, netting and fleece now.** They last 5–10 years, they are
the single most effective non-chemical pest control, and there is no substitute
for them if they cannot be bought.

# 4. Cold frame

**4 h** · **€20 (free from old windows)** · Skill ⬤○○ · **Best value structure
in the garden**

Does most of what a greenhouse does for a twentieth of the cost: hardening off,
early sowing, winter salad, overwintering, propagation, and season extension at
both ends.

```
            ╱▔▔▔▔▔▔▔▔▔▔▔▔▔▔▔▔▔▔▔  ← old window as the lid, hinged at the
          ╱   glazed lid, 15–20°      BACK, propped open with a notched stick
        ╱                             (ventilation is the main daily job)
      ┌─────────────────────────┐
      │                         │  BACK 400 mm high
      │   1200 × 600 internal   │  FRONT 250 mm high
      │                         │  Slope faces SOUTH
      └─────────────────────────┘
       sitting on soil, not concrete — the earth is your heat store
```

**Materials:** an old window, a double-glazed unit, or twin-wall polycarbonate ·
22 × 150 boards or 18 mm exterior ply · 45 × 45 corner battens · 2 hinges ·
a notched prop stick.

**Build:** a simple four-sided box sized to your window, **back higher than
front** so the lid sheds rain and faces the low winter sun · hinge the window at
the back · **make a notched prop stick with 3 or 4 notches** so you can vent it
by degrees · set it on soil against a south-facing wall if you have one (the wall
stores heat and releases it at night).

**Use it:** ⚠️ **The main risk is cooking your plants, not freezing them.** On a
sunny March day a closed cold frame reaches 40 °C+ and kills everything inside.
**Vent it every morning, close it every evening**, from February onwards. Put an
old duvet or a straw mat over it on hard frost nights. Two frames is better than
one big one — different crops want different management.

# 5. Low tunnel

**2 h** · **€40** · Skill ⬤○○

A row-scale version: hoops and a cover over one bed. Buys **2–4 weeks at each end
of the season**, protects against frost, wind, pigeons and cabbage white, and
rolls up in ten seconds.

**Build:** the same hoop construction as the insect cage (§3) — rebar sockets,
MDPE pipe hoops at 1 m centres, a ridge line — but covered with **horticultural
fleece** (frost protection, 2–4 °C, breathable, lets rain through) or **clear
polythene** (more warmth, but you must vent it, and it needs watering).

**A single set of hoops per bed, with three different covers** (insect mesh,
fleece, polythene) swapped through the year, is the most flexible and cheapest
protection system there is. Build the hoops once; change the skin by season.

# 6. Walk-in polytunnel 3 × 6 m

**2 days, 2 people** · **€500** · Skill ⬤⬤⬤

**Changes your growing calendar by 6–8 weeks at each end** and makes winter salad
possible. The highest-value structure on a food-producing property after the
root cellar.

```
                    ╱▔▔▔▔▔▔▔▔▔▔╲          Ridge 2200 mm
                  ╱              ╲
                ╱                  ╲
              ╱                      ╲    Hoops at 1200 mm centres
            ╱                          ╲  → 6 hoops over 6 m
           │                            │
           │      3000 mm span          │
           │                            │
      ═════╧════════════════════════════╧═════
       foundation tubes 600 mm into the ground
       polythene trenched in 300 mm on both sides, or on base rails

       DOORS AT BOTH ENDS — non-negotiable. A tunnel you cannot cross-ventilate
       cooks its contents in April and grows mildew all summer.
```

**Materials:** a **polytunnel kit** is usually cheaper and far better than
improvising — 25–32 mm galvanised steel hoops, foundation tubes, ridge bar,
crop bars, door frames (~€350–500 for this size) · **720 gauge (180 micron)
UV-stabilised horticultural polythene**, 5–7 year life · **anti-hot-spot tape** ·
timber for door frames · a spare polythene sheet for when this one goes.

**Build**
1. **Site it:** open, sunny, sheltered from the prevailing wind but not in a frost
   pocket. **Run it north–south** for even light. Level ground, or terrace it.
2. **Set out accurately.** Mark the rectangle and **check the diagonals are
   equal** before anything goes in the ground. Everything downstream depends on it.
3. **Foundation tubes 600 mm into the ground** at 1.2 m centres, all at the same
   height, plumb.
4. Assemble the hoops and drop them into the foundation tubes; fit the ridge bar
   and crop bars.
5. **★ Apply anti-hot-spot tape along every hoop before the polythene goes on. ★**
   Bare metal gets hot in the sun and degrades the film exactly where it touches.
   Skipping this can halve the life of a €150 cover. It is the single most
   commonly skipped step.
6. Build and fit the **door frames at both ends.**
7. **Cover on a warm, still day** — warm polythene stretches and pulls drum-tight;
   cold polythene never does, and wind makes it dangerous. Two or three people.
   **Never attempt it in a breeze.**
8. **Trench the film in**: dig a 300 mm trench down each side, pull the film
   tight, lay it in and backfill. (Or fit base rails, which make re-covering far
   easier later.)
9. Fix the film to the door frames with battens.

**Use it:** ⚠️ **Ventilation is the whole job.** Open both doors on any sunny day
from March onwards — it hits 45 °C inside on a mild spring day. Water in the
morning, at the base, never overhead. Rotate crops inside it too; tunnel soil
gets exhausted and disease-ridden fast. Mulch heavily — a tunnel dries out
extremely quickly.

**Buy the replacement polythene sheet now** and store it dry and dark. In five
years you may not be able to get one, and a tunnel without a skin is a pile of
hoops.

# 7. Seed-starting rack

**2 h** · **€80–150** · Skill ⬤○○

Starting plants indoors is what makes a short season productive. Windowsills give
leggy, weak seedlings; a simple lit rack gives stocky ones.

**Build:** a 4-tier steel or timber shelf unit (or plain shelves on brackets)
with an **LED grow bar or a plain 4000–6500 K LED batten suspended 100–150 mm
above each shelf, on adjustable chains** so you can raise it as the plants grow.
Add a **heat mat (20 W) with a thermostat** on the bottom shelf for germinating
tomatoes, peppers and aubergines, which need 20–25 °C.

**Numbers:** 20–40 W of LED per shelf, 14–16 h/day → **280–640 Wh/day per shelf.**
That is real load — plan it in your power budget
([Chapter 02](../docs/02-electricity.md)), or run it only in daylight hours off
surplus solar, or accept a windowsill and slower growth.

**Also:** a bench, seed trays and modules (buy plenty of spares — they crack),
labels and a **pencil** (ink fades in a month outdoors), a fine-rose watering can,
sieved compost, and a notebook.

# 8. Comfrey / nettle liquid feed barrel

**30 min** · **€20** · Skill ⬤○○ · **Free fertiliser, forever**

Comfrey (potassium — for tomatoes, potatoes, fruit) and nettles (nitrogen — for
leafy growth) make excellent liquid feeds from plants that grow themselves.

**Build:** a 60–200 L barrel with a **tap at the base**, on blocks. Inside, a mesh
bag or a perforated bucket holding the leaves so the liquid runs clear. A lid —
**a tight one**, because it smells genuinely appalling. Site it well away from the
house and from the neighbours.

**Method:** stuff the bag with comfrey or nettle leaves, weigh it down, fill with
water, cover · leave **3–4 weeks** · draw off from the tap and **dilute 1:10 to
1:20** until it is the colour of weak tea · water onto soil, not leaves ·
top the barrel up with fresh leaves and water as you use it.

**Concentrate version** (no water, no smell): pack a barrel with comfrey leaves
only, weighted down, with a tap at the bottom. Over a few weeks they collapse
into a **black concentrate** that drips out. **Dilute 1:40.** No water added, far
less smell, and it stores.

**Plant a dozen Bocking 14 comfrey roots** in a corner now. Cut it 3–4 times a
year. It is sterile so it will not seed itself across the garden, its roots reach
down 2 m to mine minerals nothing else can, and it is also chicken and rabbit
feed and a fine compost activator.

# 9. Leaf mould cage

**30 min** · **€15** · Skill ⬤○○ · **Free soil improver**

Autumn leaves become the best water-retaining soil conditioner there is, for
nothing but time.

**Build:** a cylinder of 1 m-high galvanised wire mesh, about 1 m across, held
with 4 stakes. Or four pallets. Or just a heap in a corner.

**Use:** fill with autumn leaves, tread down, water if dry, and **wait 1–2 years**
(leaves break down by fungal action, not bacterial — it is slow and it needs no
turning, no nitrogen and no attention). Shredding with a mower first halves the
time. Oak and beech take longest; they also make the best mould.

**Build two or three cages**, one for each year's leaves, and you get a barrow of
free soil conditioner every autumn in perpetuity. Use it as a mulch, a seed
compost ingredient, and to bulk out potting mixes when compost is scarce.
